Suppose some men are keen on purple eyes, and all the women in your hometown have them. As long as you stay in your hometown, your purple eyes don't give you any special advantage. Nor do they determine which men pursue you. But one day you move to a new town far away. In this new town you're the only woman with purple eyes. Although you haven't changed, you notice higher quality men are chasing you. Furthermore, your suitors all have something in common. They now consist entirely of men who love purple eyes.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/aig-purple2-1.jpg)

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/aig-red3.jpg)

_This example is fictional, but the principle of arbitrage applies to many real traits or groups of traits._

Moving to an environment where your standout traits have a different supply-demand ratio changes how much men value you. When all else is equal, you'll have better options where your attractive traits are scarcer. This economic principle is called arbitrage. And as we've seen, such a move doesn't just increase your value. It also changes the characteristics of your suitors. Let's try to understand why.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/aig-glasses2.jpg)

_"All my boyfriends have liked women who wear glasses."_

We've previously observed that some men like traits others are indifferent to. This point can be restated from the opposite perspective. A woman is more valuable to men who have a stronger preference for the traits that distinguish her. It follows that her best suitors will come from this group of men. In fact, when a woman has a trait that's uncommon, attractive, and important, _all_ her best suitors will be men who want that trait much more than the average man does. I call these standout traits.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/hope-45.jpg)

_"All my boyfriends have liked singers."_

Consider a pricey vacation rental on the beach. It's obvious that nearly everyone who rents it will be keen on the beach. Otherwise they wouldn't pay the extra cost to be there. And the same holds in feminine beauty.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/tuba-beach2.webp)

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/tuba-beach1.webp)

_"Why does everyone who comes here like the beach? It's so weird!"_

Landlords know their properties will rent for more when they advertise their standout traits. It would be silly to conceal the fact that a beach house is on the beach. Yet some women feel uncomfortable about advertising their standout traits and exclusively attracting suitors who prize them, especially when beauty arbitrage makes the effect salient.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/75/49/75490b33-7444-4a11-886d-0e9d3cca15d9/content/images/2025/09/dance.jpg)

_"Where I'm from everyone knows how to dance."_

Such worries are misguided. Market forces dictate that your best suitors will come precisely from this group of men. Shunning them or hiding your standout traits will only lower the quality of suitor you can attract. The same principle applies if those standout traits happen to be more common elsewhere. When basic economics hands you free money, don't leave it on the table.
