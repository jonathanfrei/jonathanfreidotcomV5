---
title: Clermont, 27 November 1095
---

In the open ground beyond the walls of Clermont, on the morning of 27 November 1095, the breath of several thousand people hung in the cold air. Bishops stood in their vestments. Abbots and monks clustered near the front. Behind them pressed knights, lesser lords, townspeople, and the curious who had walked in from the surrounding countryside of the Auvergne. Frost still clung to the edges of the field in the shadows of the walls. They had already sat through days of synodal business—simony, clerical discipline, the renewal of the Peace and Truce of God. The air smelled of woodsmoke from the town and of damp earth underfoot. Now the pope rose to speak of something else.

Urban II was a man of the French aristocracy who had become a Cluniac monk and then a reforming pope. He knew how to hold a crowd. Born Odo of Châtillon-sur-Marne, he had risen through the ranks of Cluny before Gregory VII called him to Rome, and he had spent years learning how far papal authority could reach in a world of kings, counts, and local custom. He had been moving through France since the summer, holding synods at Piacenza earlier in the year and then southward and westward, meeting local powers, settling disputes, and preparing the ground with the care of a man who understood both the possibilities and the limits of papal authority north of the Alps. Clermont lay deep in the Auvergne, outside the direct reach of the French king, in a region where reform ideas and Peace-of-God traditions already had roots. The council had opened on 18 November. For nine days it dealt with the ordinary business of a reform synod: the reform of clerical life, the protection of church property, the discipline of those who bought and sold sacred office. On the twenty-seventh, Urban addressed the larger gathering outdoors, where the press of bodies made an indoor hall impossible.

What he said that day has reached us only through later reconstructions. No notary took down his words. The four principal accounts were written years afterward by men who claimed to have heard him or to have spoken with those who had. Fulcher of Chartres, who may have been present and who later became chaplain to Baldwin of Boulogne, produced the most restrained version, closer in tone to a report than to a set piece. Robert the Monk, writing a more literary history some years later at the request of a monastery that wanted a fuller account than the sparse *Gesta Francorum*, supplies the most vivid rhetoric and the strongest emphasis on the special election of the Franks. Baldric of Dol, composing for a clerical audience, stresses the moral duty of the warrior class. Guibert of Nogent adds further moral and theological coloring and is frank about reshaping what he had heard. Each writer shaped the material for his own audience and purposes. None can be treated as a verbatim record. Read together, however, and checked against the handful of letters Urban himself wrote in the following months, they allow a reconstruction of the core that is firmer than skepticism sometimes admits.

Eastern Christians were under assault. Churches had been desecrated or destroyed. Pilgrims had been hindered or killed on the roads to the holy places. The city where Christ had died and risen was in the hands of those who did not confess His name. The warriors of the West, who spent their strength fighting one another over fields and feuds, were called to turn that strength outward. Those who went, moved by devotion rather than by the hope of gain, would receive a spiritual benefit framed as the remission of the penance due for their sins.

When he finished, the shout rose: “Deus le volt”—God wills it. Men pulled at their own clothing, tore strips of cloth, and began to fashion rough crosses. Some fixed the pieces to their shoulders at once. Others ran to find more cloth. Adhemar, bishop of Le Puy, a man already known for energy and administrative skill, and who had made a pilgrimage to the East a decade earlier, came forward. Urban named him legate and gave him authority to act in the pope’s name for the whole enterprise. The bishop would travel with the armies. The cross and the legate’s commission turned a sermon into an obligation that could move.

In the months that followed, the same message traveled with Urban on a long circuit through France—Limoges, Poitiers, Tours, and beyond—and ahead of him on the tongues of bishops, abbots, and popular preachers. Peter the Hermit and others carried a more emotional version of the appeal into the towns and villages of northern France and the Rhineland. By the spring and summer of 1096, armed companies were forming in regions that shared neither a king nor a common language. In the Limousin, in Normandy, in Flanders, in Lorraine, in Provence, men sold land, settled old quarrels with monasteries, placed their wives and children under the Church’s protection, sewed crosses to their mantles, and set out toward a city most of them would never have seen. Some left in ordered contingents under known leaders. Others joined the more chaotic popular expeditions that preceded the princely armies and met disaster in Hungary and Asia Minor. The form varied. The obligation did not. Something new had entered the life of Latin Christendom—not the sudden invention of holy war out of nothing, but a new combination of elements that no earlier summons had successfully held together and put into operation across so many polities at once.
