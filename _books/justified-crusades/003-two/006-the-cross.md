---
title: Liturgy, Preaching, and the Sign of the Cross
---

The sign that began as a strip of cloth sewn in a crowd became, over a century and a half, a rite that a bishop could perform from a book used from England to Italy. The obligation that had been shouted in an open field acquired a form that could be repeated in any parish where a licensed preacher arrived and a pontifical lay open.

At first the taking of the cross was largely informal. After Urban’s sermon men tore cloth, marked themselves, and followed the example of a legate who stepped forward where everyone could see. The vow and the visible sign together created the status of *crucesignatus*—one signed with the cross—but the written ceremonies that would later fix the act in liturgical books were still rare. Twelfth-century English and continental evidence preserves a handful of early forms; James Brundage published three English examples, and Kenneth Pennington added continental pontifical witnesses. Most practice remained closer to improvisation under a bishop’s or preacher’s eye than to a fixed sequence of prayers. What mattered legally was already clear. A public vow, once recognized, could be enforced through the courts of the Church. Privileges of protection for property and family, delay of suits, and exemption from certain burdens attached to those who had taken the cross in good faith and were preparing to fulfill it.

By the thirteenth century the ceremony had settled into the pontificals. The Roman curial rite and, later, the influential form associated with William Durandus of Mende set a pattern that local books adapted without abandoning: a short prayer blessing the cross; a formula imposing it; closing prayers that often joined the blessing of the pilgrim’s staff and scrip. The imposition language, in its mature shape, asked the recipient to accept the sign of the holy cross in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, in the image of the Passion and death of the Lord Jesus Christ, and for the defense of body and soul, so that by divine grace, after completing the journey, he might return healed and corrected. The same basic shape—bless, impose, pray—became available across Latin Christendom through the ordinary channels that carried other liturgical texts. A man who took the cross in York and a man who took it in Bari performed acts that a canonist could recognize as the same status. The rite did not erase local variation in wording or gesture. It made the variation legible within a shared form that no single kingdom controlled.

The human infrastructure that carried the call between major expeditions was the licensed preacher.

In the twelfth century Cistercians had been prominent among those authorized to stir recruitment and to frame the Passion as the reason for departure. From the 1230s the Dominicans and Franciscans dominated the work under papal license, especially after Gregory IX systematized their use for crusade preaching across Europe. Christoph Maier’s study of the mendicant campaigns shows an organization that treated Christendom as a map of languages and of recent saturation. Preachers were assigned with attention to linguistic boundaries so that the sermon could be understood without an interpreter. They were instructed not to work ground that others had recently covered, lest the same towns hear the same call until it lost force. Local bishops, often at odds with the friars over ordinary pastoral jurisdiction and burial rights, frequently cooperated when the campaign was a crusade authorized from Rome. Lent was the favored season. The setting was commonly the mass or the moments that followed it, when the Cross and the Passion already stood at the center of the liturgy. Cloth crosses were ready for immediate signing. Name-rolls recorded those who stepped forward. *Exempla*—short stories meant to hold attention, stir contrition, and move the will—supplied the emotional machinery of recruitment. Model sermon collections, including those associated with Humbert of Romans, standardized technique so that a friar trained in one province could preach a recognizable campaign in another without inventing the structure of the appeal from nothing.

The same network had to serve more than one theatre. Holy Land recovery, Baltic campaigns in support of the Teutonic Knights, and wars against heretics all competed for attention and for the spiritual rewards that drew recruits and money. The result was not a single uniform shout across Europe but a targeted, regionalized messaging system. Intensity varied with the strength of the local mendicant houses, the cooperation of the bishop, the memory of recent preaching, and the pull of rival causes. A province that had just been worked for a Baltic recruitment drive might answer a Holy Land summons more slowly. What the mendicants supplied, even so, was a pan-European human network that the twelfth century had not possessed: mobile, papally authorized, present in ordinary towns, capable of turning a papal letter into a Lenten series of sermons and a list of names. After *Quia maior* opened the gate to wider signing and to redemption, the same preachers also collected money and managed the conversion of many crosses into cash for trained fighters. The sermon and the chest belonged to the same visit.

Around the formal rite and the preaching tour grew a communal liturgy that kept the eastern project present when no army was assembling.

Parish and cathedral churches ordered processions, votive masses, and special prayers for the Holy Land as recurring features of the year. Chests with three locks—keys held by the bishop or his deputy, the parish priest, and a trusted layman—received alms destined for the same cause that the friars preached and the tenths funded. The locked box made the distant war a local fact of wood and iron. Symbolic dates could frame politics. When Frederick Barbarossa held his great diet in 1188, the liturgical association of *Laetare Jerusalem* with the occasion linked the assembly of princes to the recovery of the city whose name the introit invoked. Liturgy did not create the political decision; it gave the decision a sacred calendar and a shared vocabulary. In the field the same repertoire traveled with the host—crosses carried before the ranks, relics displayed, blessings given before combat—so that the signs that marked a man in a parish church marked the army on a foreign shore. Clergy who had preached the cross in Europe celebrated masses and heard confessions under the same theology of Passion and remission that had filled the name-rolls at home.

The spiritual economy widened with the legal and liturgical forms. Urban’s original offer had spoken of the remission of penance for those who went from devotion rather than from ambition or greed. By the thirteenth century the language of full remission for those who went in person at their own expense, properly confessed, stood alongside proportionate spiritual benefit for those who sent proxies, gave money, or redeemed vows they could not fulfill in person. *Quia maior* and the legislation of the Fourth Lateran Council made that expansion explicit. The crusade was no longer only a career for fighting elites. It was embedded in the ordinary penitential and devotional life of Latin Christians who might never leave their diocese. A widow’s legacy, a merchant’s alms, a knight’s redeemed vow, and a fighter’s departure all drew on a common vocabulary of the cross and of remission.

The ritual system had limits as real as those of the taxes, the orders, and the ships.

Preaching could intensify while the number of men who actually departed fell. Ritual mobilization without projection produced signed crosses, locked chests, and sermons that did not put a host on the water. Formal rites and the privileges attached to them did not guarantee that vows would be fulfilled. Canon law could pursue those who took the cross and then settled back into ordinary life, but pursuit was uneven, and many simply faded from the rolls without a recorded redemption or a recorded departure. The extension of spiritual benefit to donors and redeemers kept money moving and non-combatants inside the enterprise, yet it also risked turning the cross into a transaction—a payment for a privilege rather than a promise of departure. Critics noticed. Matthew Paris and others attacked the friars’ role in collecting redemptions even when they conceded that the collectors often acted under formal papal privileges. The papacy issued rules against abuse without ever closing the gap between norm and practice. Local intensity varied widely. Competing theatres blunted response. A region recently saturated with preaching, or drawn toward a Baltic or anti-heretical campaign, might answer a Holy Land summons with less force than the letters from Rome assumed. The same portable sign that coordinated could also be emptied of the journey it was meant to begin.

None of that erased what the liturgical and preaching apparatus did. Between the great departures, and underneath them, the same sign, the same basic vow language, and a network of licensed voices kept the obligation legible from one end of Latin Christendom to the other. A parish that had heard a friar in Lent, kept a chest for the Holy Land, and watched a neighbor take the cross under a formula that a bishop in another kingdom would have recognized, inhabited a shared institutional world that ordinary dynastic politics did not supply. The rite could be empty. The sermon could be ignored. The combination of rite, sermon, and standing spiritual economy still made the next summons thinkable in places that had never seen a royal host assemble on their own soil.

Money that could be raised under a shared claim, corporations that outlasted any single host, ships and contracts that made repetition organizationally feasible, and a portable sacred sign with its rite and its preachers: the joints of the coordinating machinery were now all in view. What they did together, and where they failed together, remains to be measured as a single system.
