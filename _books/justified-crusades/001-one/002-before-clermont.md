---
title: Before Clermont
deck: Precedents and Their Limits
---

For generations before that morning at Clermont, Latin Christians had fought under religious banners. The practice was neither rare nor clandestine. In the ninth century, when Muslim raiders from North Africa and Spain threatened the coasts of Italy and even the approaches to Rome, popes had appealed for help and spoken of spiritual reward for those who fell in defense of the Roman see. Leo IV had fortified the Leonine City after the Saracen raid of 846 and been present when Christian forces met Saracen ships at the mouth of the Tiber. John VIII, writing in the later ninth century, had spoken of heavenly recompense for those who died fighting the enemies of the Church. These were real grants of spiritual privilege, rooted in the defense of Christian territory and of the papal city itself. A warrior who answered such a call still fought under the authority of a local ruler or in defense of a specific place. He did not join a general enterprise of Latin Christendom as a whole, directed toward a distant goal under a shared sacred obligation that crossed the boundaries of kingdoms.

The eleventh-century reform papacy raised the stakes. Leo IX took the field in person against the Normans of southern Italy in 1053 and offered German troops a form of remission for their service. The battle of Civitate ended in papal defeat and captivity, but the precedent of a pope directing and spiritually rewarding military action was set. Alexander II extended encouragement and spiritual benefit to warriors fighting in Spain—most famously in connection with the expedition against Barbastro in 1064—and to the Norman conquest of Muslim Sicily under the brothers Hauteville. The language of recovery—of restoring to the Church what had once been Christian—began to circulate with greater confidence. In Spain the reconquest of territory lost centuries earlier could be framed as restoration. In Sicily the same language justified Norman ambition under a papal banner. Gregory VII went further still. He spoke of a *militia Sancti Petri*, a knighthood of Saint Peter, and sought lasting commitments from selected princes and nobles. In the mid-1070s he contemplated an expedition that would first settle affairs in Italy and then cross to aid Eastern Christians against the Turks who had broken Byzantine power at Manzikert in 1071. He wrote to Count William of Upper Burgundy asking for knights who would serve the Roman see. He explored similar understandings with other lords, including the possibility of leading an expedition in person. The idea of papally directed military service on a significant scale was no longer unthinkable. It had been discussed, negotiated, and in limited regional cases put into practice.

Historians have long debated how sharp a break 1095 represents. Carl Erdmann and those who followed him traced a long development of holy-war ideas within the Latin Church and tended to see Clermont as the culmination of a process already well advanced in the reform papacy’s experiments in directed violence and in the ideology of recovery visible in Spain and Sicily. Other scholars have insisted more strongly on discontinuity: the novelty of the general summons addressed to the arms-bearing class as such, the penitential reframing of the fighting itself, and the multi-polity response that actually followed. The disagreement is not merely semantic. Continuity readings risk understating how limited and regional earlier papal military initiatives remained; discontinuity readings risk treating 1095 as a clean rupture when the conceptual materials were already circulating. Both have force. The test that matters for the argument here is operational rather than purely conceptual: whether a general call treating the arms-bearing population of many kingdoms as a single reservoir of force, attached to a spiritual reward that redefined fighting itself as a penitential act, and given institutional form through vow, visible sign, and early legal protection, had ever before been successfully put into practice across multiple polities toward a distant sacred goal. On that narrower test, earlier pieces existed. The combination in successful operation did not.

The Spanish and Sicilian campaigns remained regional. They recovered territory that had been Christian; they enjoyed papal approval and, in some cases, explicit spiritual privileges; but they were still framed as the enterprises of particular rulers and their followers. A knight who fought at Barbastro did so under a count or a duke whose political interests shaped the campaign and who expected a share of the spoils. A Norman who fought in Sicily served the ambitions of Robert Guiscard and his brothers as much as any abstract recovery of the Church. Gregory’s projected eastern expedition never moved beyond correspondence and negotiation. His *militia Sancti Petri* was a network of personal bonds between the papacy and selected lords, not a mobilizing claim addressed to Latin Christendom as a whole. When warriors from different regions cooperated, they did so under a king, a great magnate, or a temporary alliance of convenience. They did not move because a shared sacred duty had been laid upon them by the bishop of Rome and recognized across the boundaries of kingdoms as binding on the arms-bearing class as such.

A different kind of precedent existed in the Peace and Truce of God. From the late tenth century onward, in assemblies across southern and central France—at Charroux, Narbonne, Toulouges, and many other places—clergy and laity had sworn collective oaths to protect the unarmed—peasants, merchants, clerics, women—and to suspend private warfare during certain seasons and on certain days. These councils demonstrated that the Church could gather large numbers of people, extract public promises, and attempt to discipline the violence of the military class. The rituals were vivid. Relics were brought out from the churches and set upon the open ground. Oaths were sworn on the bones of saints. Excommunication hung over those who broke the peace. Bishops and abbots used the fear of divine punishment and the hope of divine protection to restrain the feuding of castellans and knights. Urban knew this tradition from the inside. The Council of Clermont itself renewed and extended the Peace. But the Peace and Truce remained instruments of internal order. They sought to limit the damage knights did to their own society—to churches, to harvests, to the defenseless. They did not generate long-distance military projection. They did not invite the warrior elites of multiple polities to recognize a common external obligation that overrode ordinary rivalries for years at a time. They were a school in collective oath-taking and ecclesiastical discipline. They were not a template for a pan-Christian expedition to Jerusalem.

Byzantine requests for Western fighters were likewise familiar by 1095. Emperors had recruited Frankish and Norman mercenaries for decades. After Manzikert, the need grew more urgent. Alexios I Komnenos’s envoys appeared at the Council of Piacenza in March of that year asking for help against the Turks who had overrun much of Asia Minor. The request belonged to an established pattern of Byzantine diplomacy: the empire sought soldiers, preferably under imperial command, to recover lost provinces. It supplied Urban with an occasion and a measure of urgency. It did not dictate the shape of what followed. The emperor wanted auxiliaries. The pope eventually offered something larger and differently conceived: a general call framed as the concern of Latin Christian society as a whole, with a spiritual reward that redefined the act of fighting itself, and with institutional form through vow, visible sign, and early legal protection.

Latin Christendom therefore already possessed ideas of just war and of holy war, practices of pilgrimage to the eastern holy places, mechanisms for collective oath-taking under ecclesiastical pressure, and occasional papal grants of spiritual privilege for specific campaigns. What it did not yet possess, in successful operation, was a general call that treated the warrior elites of many kingdoms as bound by a shared sacred duty to a distant goal under papal direction. That combination appeared in the months after Clermont.
