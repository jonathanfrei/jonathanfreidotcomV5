The earlier account of causes described how culture makes some adult lives appear normal, admirable, or safely postponable. The institutional question is who gives those judgments a visible form. Employers confer status, families recommend or withhold a model of adulthood, congregations celebrate promises and births, and stories teach young people what success looks like.
{: .lede}

Parents know that children are costly in the broadest sense. They interrupt sleep, strain a marriage, and narrow freedom for years. In a 2023 Pew survey, parents commonly said the work was harder than expected, yet most also described it as rewarding and enjoyable most of the time. Public language has become better at describing the loss of freedom than the enlargement of life that many parents report alongside it.

The same survey found that American parents overwhelmingly wanted their children to become financially independent and find enjoyable work, while far fewer placed comparable importance on marriage or children. Some of that reluctance is kindness: a parent does not want to pressure a daughter who may be infertile or a son who has not found a spouse. It can also leave young adults with a strange message: family was good enough to organize their parents' lives around, but too private a good to recommend.

Naming a good is not the same as compelling it. We tell young people that friendship and health are worth seeking without despising people who are lonely or ill. Marriage and parenthood can be spoken of with the same confidence.

## Honor changes the calculation

Money reduces a cost. Honor changes what a person is willing to pay. A woman who steps away from paid work to raise three children gives up income and professional status that the culture knows how to measure. Calling her “not working” makes the labor inside the home disappear from view.

Public honor can be ordinary. An employer can treat a returning mother as an experienced adult rather than a stalled employee. A father who turns down a promotion because he wants to remain present to his wife and children can still look successful. Stories can portray good parents as competent people with interior lives rather than as exhausted warnings to everyone behind them.

Honor without material support is cheap. Praising motherhood while structuring work around permanent availability asks women to accept the cost and everyone else to enjoy the sentiment. Praising fatherhood while expecting men to outsource care to their wives does the same. The institutions described in the previous sections give cultural language something real to name.

Simple tolerance is not enough to reverse a culture that attaches prestige to autonomy, consumption, and uninterrupted career ascent. Culture teaches through aspiration. It places some lives on magazine covers, in advertisements, on screens, and in the stories young people tell about success. If family formation is going to recover, motherhood and fatherhood will have to become visibly aspirational again. A woman with four children should be able to look accomplished because of the family she has built, not despite it. A man whose life is organized around being a husband and father should not appear to have settled for less.

This kind of counter-signal need not denigrate other lives. It does require abandoning the fiction that a healthy culture must be neutral among every model of adulthood. Societies already celebrate some choices more than others. A family-oriented culture would deliberately give marriage, motherhood, fatherhood, and larger families more honor, beauty, and status than they receive now.

That power can go wrong. Praise can become surveillance of a woman's body, pressure on an infertile couple, concealment of violence, or mockery of an unmarried adult. A culture may honor marriage without pretending every marriage is safe. It may admire motherhood without treating paid work as betrayal, and admire a woman's public work without treating children as an impediment she should have avoided. Invitation names a good and helps people pursue it. Shame turns another person's wound or refusal into a public instrument. The next chapter will examine stronger efforts to direct behavior; they do not belong smuggled into this affirmative case.

Ritual makes the judgment visible. Weddings announce that a private promise has witnesses. A baptism or meal after a birth tells parents that the people around them see a family beginning and intend to remain. A government campaign can praise an abstraction called “the family.” A ritual knows the child's name.

Adults who do not marry or have children do not become less valuable. Some choose that life, some receive it unwillingly, and many serve the next generation through adoption, teaching, friendship, kinship, craft, discovery, or care. These forms of fruitfulness are real. They do not produce biological succession, and biological parenthood does not exhaust the work of handing on a civilization. A culture confident about parenthood has no need to humiliate anyone. Contempt would corrupt the good being defended by reducing human worth to demographic output.

## Confidence without denial

Children tie a parent to a future he will not control. Economic pessimism can therefore affect births before income actually falls. Climate anxiety makes the fear explicit. Research in three European countries found that intense climate worry was associated with a higher likelihood of intending no children rather than two.

Concern for the future is reasonable. A person worried about climate change may refuse parenthood, or may understand raising a child as a commitment to repair a world that child will inherit. The association between worry and intentions does not decide which response is rational. Confidence does not require believing the future will be easy. It means believing that courage, skill, restraint, and love will remain useful in an imperfect world.

The healthier cultural voice does not deny cost or uncertainty. It tells the truth about both and still says that marriage, motherhood, fatherhood, and the work of raising children belong to a good life. Children should grow up hearing that their arrival did more than complicate one. They should also see adults beyond their parents taking responsibility for a future they may not live to enjoy. Honor begins the work of transmission; repeated practice completes it.
