A couple can bring a first baby home with two incomes, health insurance, parental leave, and a government child benefit and still discover at 2:30 in the morning that they are mostly on their own. The baby is sick, one parent has a meeting at eight, and the grandparents live six states away; on paper, the family is well supported. In practice, there is no one to call.

**Someone near enough to help** A grandmother carrying an infant, 2015. Policy can protect time and lower a cost; it cannot create the person who is close enough to take a turn. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grandmother_and_grandchild.jpg)*

The leave and benefit still matter. They protect income and time at a moment when both are scarce. The gap appears because a payment cannot become the person who takes the next shift: the friend who introduced the couple, the grandfather who collects a sick child, the older couple whose marriage makes commitment look ordinary rather than heroic.

Modern life has made adulthood more individually achievable and family life more individually burdensome. Education, work, and housing are organized around a person moving freely toward the best opportunity. Families depend on something less efficient: people remaining near one another long enough to become responsible for each other.

That older interdependence was not innocent. It could trap a woman in unpaid labor, keep an adult child near controlling parents, conceal abuse, and exclude anyone who did not fit. Mobility and independence protect real freedoms. The task is to recover commitment, proximity, and shared care without recovering domination.

This chapter's practical hypothesis is that renewal must begin along the whole path ordinary policy usually enters late: meeting, commitment, housing, marriage, pregnancy, birth, recovery, care, work, another child, and long-term security. Many adults still want more family than they expect to have. Making that path inhabitable is therefore the humane first task, even though wanted-family attainment is not the final measure of renewal.

No single institution performs that whole sequence. Friends, families, churches, schools, and civic groups help people meet; couples make commitments; law gives marriage public form; builders, landlords, and cities make room and proximity possible; fathers and mothers share pregnancy's demands and the work of care; healthcare and insurers protect health and recovery; employers protect time and reentry; extended family and congregations supply nearby help; public policy funds a floor of safety and professional support. The question is what each can do, who supplies the labor, how the arrangement can fail, and which protections allow dependence to be chosen rather than imposed. Renewal needs institutions that make family life easier to begin and sustain while preserving the freedom modern life rightly gained.
