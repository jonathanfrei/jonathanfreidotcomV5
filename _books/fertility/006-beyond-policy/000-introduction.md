A couple can bring a first baby home with two incomes, health insurance, parental leave, and a government child benefit and still discover at 2:30 in the morning that they are mostly on their own. The baby is sick, one parent has a meeting at eight, and the grandparents live six states away; on paper, the family is well supported. In practice, there is no one to call.

**Someone near enough to help** A grandmother carrying an infant, 2015. Policy can protect time and lower a cost; it cannot create the person who is close enough to take a turn. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grandmother_and_grandchild.jpg)*
{: .caption}

That gap is where policy reaches its limit. Governments can lower a cost or protect time away from work, but they cannot create the people who make family life less brittle: the friend who introduces a future spouse, the grandmother who picks up a sick child, the older couple whose marriage makes commitment look ordinary rather than heroic.

Modern life has made adulthood more individually achievable and family life more individually burdensome. Education, work, and housing are increasingly organized around a person moving freely toward the best opportunity; families depend on something less efficient, people remaining near one another long enough to become useful to each other.

Family formation is the more promising place to look for renewal. Many adults still want more family than they ultimately have, so reversal begins before the birth benefit and often before pregnancy: with a workable path into adulthood, relationships that become durable in time, and communities where dependence is not treated as failure. Recovering these functions does not require recreating an older social order. It requires forms that fit modern freedom and make family life easier to begin and sustain.
