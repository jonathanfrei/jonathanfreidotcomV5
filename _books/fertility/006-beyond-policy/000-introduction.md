A couple can bring a first baby home with two incomes, health insurance, parental leave, and a government child benefit and still discover at 2:30 in the morning that they are mostly on their own. The baby is sick, one parent has a meeting at eight, and the grandparents live six states away. On paper, the family is well supported. In practice, there is no one to call.
{: .lede}

That gap is where policy reaches its limit. Governments can lower a cost or protect time away from work. They cannot create the people who make family life less brittle: the friend who introduces a future spouse, the grandmother who picks up a sick child, the older couple whose marriage makes commitment look ordinary rather than heroic.

Modern life has made adulthood more individually achievable and family life more individually burdensome. Education, work, and housing are increasingly organized around a person moving freely toward the best opportunity. Families depend on something less efficient: people remaining near one another long enough to become useful to each other.

Many adults still want more family than they ultimately have. Reversal therefore begins before the birth benefit and often before pregnancy. It begins with a workable path into adulthood, relationships that become durable in time, and communities where dependence is not treated as failure. The task is not to recreate an older social order. It is to recover the functions that older arrangements sometimes supplied and find forms that fit modern freedom better.
