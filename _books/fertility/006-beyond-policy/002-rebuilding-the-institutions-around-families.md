Two parents were never meant to be a complete institution. A newborn exposes the fiction quickly. Someone has to hold the baby while the mother sleeps. Later someone has to collect an older child when the carefully arranged day falls apart. Family life becomes less brittle when there is backup.
{: .lede}

Grandparents often provide it. Their help may be regular childcare, but its value is larger than the hours recorded. A nearby grandmother is insurance against the call from school at 11:20 on a Tuesday. Her presence changes what a couple believes it can survive.

Linked Israeli census and population-register data offer an unusually sharp measure. The death of a grandparent reduced an adult child's probability of having a birth during the next five years by about five percentage points. Grief may affect births directly, and no grandparent should be valued as an input into another person's fertility. The finding still makes something visible that standard family policy often misses: another trusted adult can change whether a second or third child feels manageable.

Proximity cannot be ordered from an office. Adult children move for jobs, grandparents have obligations of their own, and some families are estranged for good reasons. Employers and housing markets nevertheless shape the distance. A labor market that sends every ambitious young adult toward the highest salary may gain efficient matching and lose the aunt who could have made a second child possible.

That trade can be easy to misprice. A larger paycheck appears in the household budget. The nearby sibling who can stay with a sick child does not. For some families, a smaller apartment near grandparents may be more supportive than a larger house far away. Families experience doors and travel time, not merely housing units.

## Places where families meet

Support begins before a child exists. People need places where they repeatedly encounter potential spouses, married couples, and families a few years ahead of them. A congregation can do all three. In a British cohort study, churchgoing mothers reported more durable support from fellow congregants, and that aid was associated with larger family size. Belief arrived partly as another person willing to help.

A weekly gathering also gives unmarried adults a social world larger than a dating app. Friends introduce friends. Older couples make marriage look ordinary. Young adults spend enough time around children to see that parenthood is difficult without being incomprehensible. A person who has barely held a baby can imagine parenthood as an abstract loss of freedom; someone who has watched a friend's toddler grow up has more evidence to work with.

Schools and neighborhoods can create the same repeated contact in quieter ways. Parents become friends at pickup. A playground gives people somewhere to remain after the scheduled activity ends. Weak ties become useful because they are encountered again.

Many modern places are excellent at serving customers and poor at producing members. A gym or childcare center can provide a good service while asking almost nothing durable of the people inside it. Membership is slower and occasionally inconvenient. It means showing up when the program is dull and helping someone who may never become a close friend. That mild obligation is precisely what makes the group available when life stops being convenient.

## Marriage needs company

Marriage begins with two people, but the path toward it is social. Friends can introduce a couple and make commitment seem normal. Families and clergy can notice trouble before it becomes a private catastrophe. Technique helps less than we sometimes hope. A federal evaluation of Building Strong Families found no overall improvement in relationship quality or stability after three years. Communication skills are useful; a class cannot manufacture trust around a couple who has none.

Early parenthood is when marriage can shrink into logistics. The mother is recovering, both parents are tired, and ordinary resentments acquire the moral urgency of 3 a.m. A meal or an afternoon of childcare gives the couple more than rest. It reminds them that their marriage belongs to people who want it to endure.

Rebuilding these supports will not look like one national design. A parish, an extended family, a neighborhood school, and an employer know different people and fail in different ways. That variety is a strength. The point is to recover enough redundancy that family life no longer depends on two adults performing every role without backup.
