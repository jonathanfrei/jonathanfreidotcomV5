Civilization is handed down at close range. A child learns a language because someone answers the same question again; he learns that promises bind by watching adults keep one when leaving would be easier. Much later, a young worker learns the part of a trade that never made it into the manual because an older one is still there to point it out.
{: .lede}

Archives preserve information; people preserve judgment. A recipe can survive in a database, but the feel of the dough usually requires a hand beside yours. A maintenance procedure can list the correct steps while the veteran technician hears a noise and knows something is wrong before the sensor does. Modern civilization depends on both kinds of knowledge.

Family transmission can carry vice as faithfully as skill. Cruelty and fear move between generations too. Tradition deserves attention because it may contain knowledge, not immunity from judgment. Sometimes receiving an inheritance faithfully means repairing it.

The family nevertheless has a priority that schools, markets, and governments do not. It is the first community most people enter and the place where dependence is least surprising: a baby contributes nothing to the household budget and still belongs completely. Parents and kin know a child's history, habits, fears, and loyalties in a way a distant administrator cannot. They also acquire obligations that cannot be reduced to delivering a service.

This priority sets a limit on public power without making the household sovereign. Government should protect and assist families when they cannot carry a burden alone, enforce the rights of people within them, and supply goods that local affection cannot. It should not absorb responsibilities that parents, relatives, and local communities can fulfill better. The principle often called subsidiarity begins with this practical difference in knowledge and obligation. The *Compendium of the Social Doctrine of the Church* gives one developed account of it, but the public reason does not depend on accepting that authority: responsibility should remain close to the person unless scale, justice, safety, or incapacity requires a larger institution.

## Freedom receives a world

Reproductive freedom protects a deeply personal part of human life from coercion. A woman's dignity precedes every population target, and a child is not a unit owed to the state. The damage caused by coercive population policy should make any proposed reversal suspicious of centralized power.

Free choices still accumulate into a shared world. If enough people have no children, the local orchestra loses a section and the local trade has fewer apprentices. After several generations, practices that everyone was individually free to leave have fewer people available to inherit them. Law should leave broad room for agency. Families and communities can speak about obligations that law would corrupt by enforcing.

Parents begin that transmission in small ways. Chores, apologies, promises, and gradually granted responsibility teach a child how freedom can become self-command rather than appetite. That work looks far removed from constitutional government or technological progress. Both eventually depend on adults who can be trusted when nobody is watching.

## More than preservation

Low fertility weakens transmission before it destroys information. A family business may have no child who wants the trade; a congregation can maintain a beautiful building while the habits that made it useful disappear with its oldest members. The problem is succession: who will care enough to become responsible for what was handed down?

Biological descent is only one path of transmission. Adoption creates fully real families. Immigration can bring willing successors. Teachers, friends, religious communities, workshops, and civic institutions can pass knowledge and responsibility across lines of ancestry. Every path still requires a younger person and an older one who remains long enough to teach. None eliminates the need for a generation to be born somewhere.

Transmission is not merely conservation. A fruitful institution welcomes people who were not already inside, forms their judgment, corrects what has gone wrong, and gives them room to create. The apprentice should eventually improve the method. The child should be able to repair an injustice inherited with the family story. This chapter's work ends with that formation. The final act will ask what happens when repeated contraction leaves institutions selecting among too few possible successors.

## Institutions that can bear correction

Renewal is not a return to 1955. Older arrangements sometimes supplied proximity and mutual aid by denying women economic independence, protecting violent husbands, enforcing racial or religious exclusion, or making departure ruinous. Modern mobility, paid work, law, and professional care corrected real injustice. Any institution worth rebuilding must be able to receive those corrections rather than asking people to forget why they left.

We do not know the exact arrangement of work, housing, friendship, marriage, and community that will fit a wealthy technological society and also support larger families. That uncertainty argues for experimentation. Employers can try career paths that tolerate uneven years. Cities can permit homes that let grandparents and adult children live near one another with separate doors. Congregations and civic groups can become more deliberate about introducing unmarried people and supporting new parents. Families can decide that proximity is sometimes worth more than the highest possible salary.

Some experiments will fail. Their failure should be visible. A useful pilot names the function it intends to restore, who does the work, what it costs, who is excluded, whether members can leave, and what outcome would justify continuing it. Births may be one outcome, but so may marriages formed, parents retained in work, emergencies covered, loneliness reduced, children cared for, or apprentices trained. National policy can fund and compare these efforts without pretending that a grant creates belonging.

The path through this chapter began with meeting and commitment, then moved through household, birth, care, formation, belonging, and transmission. Policy can protect each step. It cannot perform all of them. Families and institutions must make promises, accept dependence, share work, correct failure, and remain long enough to hand something on.

This repair is necessary, and it will be slow. A generation cannot be hurried into adulthood, a marriage cannot be mass-produced, and trust does not arrive on a fiscal schedule. What, then, may a society do if voluntary institutional renewal is too partial to stop continued contraction? The next chapter considers stronger measures. The standard will not be whether they can produce births alone, but whether they preserve the freedom and human goods renewal is supposed to serve.
