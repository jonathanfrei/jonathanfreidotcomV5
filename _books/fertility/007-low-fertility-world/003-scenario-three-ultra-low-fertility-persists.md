Ultra-low fertility can feel surprisingly normal at first. Trains run. A small class gets plenty of attention from its teacher. Grandparents may have more money and time for the one grandchild they have. The strangeness appears slowly, as ordinary roles and places begin to lose successors.
{: .lede}

Nearly one fifth of countries and areas had fertility below 1.4 births per woman in 2024, a range the United Nations calls ultra-low. For the 24 countries in that group whose populations had already peaked, the UN [estimated the probability of returning to replacement within thirty years at one tenth of one percent](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/wpp2024_summary_of_results_final_web.pdf). Persistence is not a prediction of doom. It is a plausible path for countries already living at rates that would make each generation dramatically smaller than the last.

The problem eventually becomes succession. A larger society leaves behind roads, hospitals, companies, parishes, family obligations, skills, and memories. Each contraction leaves fewer possible heirs, and those heirs have to decide what they can still carry.

## A narrower family

Start at a family table. An only child marries another only child, and they have one child. That child has parents and perhaps four loving grandparents, but no siblings, no aunts or uncles, and no first cousins. The family tree can be tall with living generations and almost empty across each branch.

Small families aren't defective families, and friendship can become real kinship. The structure still changes the distribution of obligation. When two parents grow frail, there is no brother to call. When the next generation has a baby, there may be no aunt nearby who has already raised one. A holiday that once gathered cousins around several tables can become a much smaller affair without anyone having deliberately chosen to end the tradition.

The same narrowing appears outside the home. [Decline first becomes visible where population was already thin](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/classifying-shrinking-regions_456afa64/0185fe09-en.pdf). A town combines classrooms and puts children on a longer bus route. Its hospital becomes an outpatient center. Empty houses stop being bargains when buyers cannot get a loan for a building expected to lose value. The remaining residents live among infrastructure built for people who left or were never born.

The central city can look healthy for a long time. It collects the region's students, doctors, and ambitious young adults, preserving excellent institutions while the surrounding towns empty. Under persistent contraction, consolidation eventually reaches the center too. A country cannot keep moving young people from one declining province to another and create more of them.

## Choosing the inheritance

At very low fertility, institutions begin making the same choice as families: what gets a successor? A university keeps the engineering department and closes a small language program. A church combines parishes and preserves one old building rather than three. A manufacturer automates its profitable line while the craft that required years of apprenticeship quietly disappears.

Each decision may be sensible. That is what makes the process difficult to notice as a whole. A shrinking civilization does not have to forget everything at once. It edits itself. The skills with obvious demand and the places with enough people survive first. Knowledge that depends on redundancy, apprenticeship, or a small community of practice is easier to lose.

AI and automation could make this world much more capable than its population suggests. One person may supervise work that once required ten. Software can preserve procedures and make expertise available almost anywhere. That may allow a country to keep excellent hospitals, factories, and public services with far fewer workers. It can also make continued contraction easier to live with, delaying the moment when anyone feels compelled to change direction.

Countries will not age together. For a time, younger regions can supply workers and markets to older ones. As fertility falls in regions that remain young today, that pool narrows too. A wealthy old country may compete successfully for migrants and use machines to compensate for missing workers. A poorer country with a young population gains no automatic competence from youth, though it has more potential workers, parents, customers, and recruits if it can educate them and govern well.

Over one generation, ultra-low fertility looks like an aging problem. Over five, it changes kinship and the national map. Ten generations of repeated contraction would produce a society unlike any modern country we know, selected down through continual choices about which places, skills, and obligations still receive successors. That is illustration, not forecast; people can change course long before then.

Human beings would still fall in love, raise children, build beautiful places, and invent substitutes for what became scarce. This is not an empty planet arriving on schedule. It is a world in which fewer people inherit more than they can carry whole. The question becomes how much can be selected away before the society loses not only pieces of its inheritance, but the spare capacity to choose a different future.
