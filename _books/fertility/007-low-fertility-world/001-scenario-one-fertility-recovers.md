Imagine that fertility turns upward and keeps going. A decade from now the maternity ward is busier. The elementary school reopens a classroom it once expected to close. The town is rebuilding its youngest generation. It still cannot find enough nurses in their thirties, and the parents filling that kindergarten may also be arranging care for their own aging parents.
{: .lede}

This is the future I hope for. It is not a quick rescue. The missing cohorts remain missing, and a baby born into the recovery will spend roughly two decades consuming care, education, and household income before entering the full-time workforce. Recovery changes the destination before it relieves the journey.

No government has yet produced a durable return from very low fertility to replacement. The [United Nations found historical rebounds in 39 countries and territories](https://population.un.org/wpp/Publications/Files/WPP2024_Methodology-Report_Final.pdf), though its model does not assume those rebounds continue above replacement. In countries whose populations have already peaked, its central projection reaches only about 1.4 births per woman in 2100. A genuine recovery would therefore be something new enough that we should be careful about declaring one too early.

## A rebound that lasts

The first encouraging number could be misleading. When births postponed during a recession or pandemic occur a year later, the total fertility rate can jump even if women eventually have about the same number of children. Researchers comparing ten wealthy countries found that later births made up some of the delay, but [the catch-up was usually too small to restore completed fertility](https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/48/15). A few good years would be welcome. They wouldn't settle the question.

A durable recovery appears in cohorts. Women born in one decade finish their childbearing years with more children than the cohort before them, and younger cohorts continue the rise. Fewer people reach their late thirties needing both a relationship and luck to deliver a first child quickly. This takes years to see clearly, which is an awkward feature of demographic repair: the evidence arrives long after the choices that produced it.

The path needn't reproduce the baby boom. Replacement is a reasonable long-run direction because it lets generations renew themselves without requiring permanent inflows from somewhere else. Moving from 1.2 to 1.7 would still recover a great many births and families. It would also leave each generation smaller than the one before it if the rate stayed there.

## The long return

Suppose fertility reached replacement next year. Five years later, the first visible effect would be in classrooms and childcare. Twenty-five years later, more of that cohort would begin appearing in workplaces. Around the same time, some would start families of their own. Only decades after the original turn would the age structure begin to look substantially healthier.

During the passage, the country has to support both ends of life. A revived elementary school may have a busy kindergarten while its town still lacks middle-aged doctors and electricians. A couple with three young children may be helping four elderly parents because the generation between the baby boom and the recovery was small. Automation, productivity, immigration, and later retirement still have work to do. The recovery does not make those adaptations unnecessary. It gives them somewhere to lead.

After five generations, the picture is different. A stable run of renewing cohorts can rebuild age balance and give a town confidence that reopening a school is not merely postponing its closure. The country may remain smaller and older than it would have been without the decline. Its future is no longer narrowing by default.

That is the odd shape of demographic recovery. The birth registry turns first. The school notices later. The labor market waits much longer. A society choosing renewal accepts costs now for people who cannot repay them for decades, and many of the adults making the choice will never see the age structure fully repaired. That is what rebuilding across generations looks like.
