# Writing Well

This guide establishes the writing-quality standard for content produced by the content factory. It is inspired by the principles William Zinsser presents in *On Writing Well*, adapted for contemporary nonfiction, technical, analytical, and editorial writing.

The goal is not to imitate Zinsser's voice. It is to apply his underlying discipline: clarity, simplicity, humanity, precision, and ruthless revision.

## 1. Know what you are trying to say

Before improving a sentence, know what the sentence is trying to accomplish.

Before improving a paragraph, know why the paragraph exists.

Before improving an article, be able to state its central idea in one sentence.

A strong nonfiction piece should leave the reader with one clear, worthwhile thought. Additional facts and arguments should support that thought rather than compete with it.

Ask:

- What am I trying to say?
- Why does it matter?
- What does the reader need to understand before this point makes sense?
- Does every major section contribute to the central idea?

If the answer to the first question is unclear, do not compensate with more prose. Clarify the thinking first.

## 2. Put clarity before style

Good writing should be easy to understand without being simplistic.

Do not make a sentence complicated merely because the subject is complicated. Technical subjects can be explained clearly. Sophisticated ideas do not require sophisticated-sounding language.

Prefer:

> The system failed because the test did not cover this case.

Over:

> The failure was attributable to a deficiency in test coverage pertaining to this particular use case.

Use the simplest construction that expresses the intended meaning accurately.

## 3. Cut the clutter

Every word should earn its place.

Look aggressively for:

- unnecessary adjectives and adverbs
- redundant phrases
- throat-clearing
- inflated terminology
- repeated conclusions
- obvious statements
- filler introductions
- unnecessary qualifiers
- phrases that merely announce what the sentence is about

Common warning signs include:

- "It is important to note that..."
- "It should be noted that..."
- "In today's rapidly changing world..."
- "At the end of the day..."
- "In order to..."
- "A wide range of..."
- "The fact that..."

Do not automatically delete a phrase because it is on a list. Delete it when it contributes nothing.

A useful editing question is:

> If I remove this word or sentence, does anything important change?

If not, cut it.

## 4. Prefer strong, specific words

Use concrete verbs and nouns instead of abstract or inflated language.

Prefer:

- use over utilize
- start over commence
- help over facilitate
- change over implement a change to
- decide over make a determination
- improve over optimize, when improvement is what is actually meant

Be especially careful with business and technical jargon. Specialized terminology is useful when it names a precise concept. It is harmful when it merely makes ordinary ideas sound more important.

Prefer specific nouns over empty placeholders. Recurring **something**, **thing**, and **things** usually mean the sentence has not yet named what it is talking about. Replace them with the actual object, claim, power, monument, machine, or consequence whenever the precise word is known.

Do not advertise significance with words such as **interesting**. If a point matters, state the point. "The interesting thing is…" is almost always weaker than the substance that should follow it.

## 5. Write like a person

The reader should hear a human mind thinking on the page.

Use natural language. Use contractions when they fit. Use first person when it makes the writing more direct or honest. State opinions as opinions rather than hiding them behind passive constructions.

Do not manufacture an artificial "writer's voice."

Do not make prose breezy, clever, academic, corporate, or inspirational merely because the subject seems to call for it.

The desired voice is clear, intelligent, curious, direct, and human.

## 6. Avoid clichés and borrowed language

Clichés are often evidence that the writer has stopped looking for the precise thing they mean.

Avoid tired phrases such as:

- "think outside the box"
- "move the needle"
- "game changer"
- "at the end of the day"
- "tip of the iceberg"
- "a double-edged sword"
- "paradigm shift"
- "unprecedented times"

The problem is not that these phrases are forbidden. The problem is that they often substitute for an original observation.

When a familiar phrase appears, ask whether there is a more precise way to say what is actually happening.

## 7. Make the writing sound good

Readers read with their eyes but also hear prose internally.

Read important passages aloud.

Listen for:

- awkward rhythm
- repeated words
- excessive sentence length
- monotonous sentence structure
- accidental rhyme
- clumsy transitions
- phrases that sound unnatural when spoken

Vary sentence length deliberately. A sequence of long sentences can exhaust a reader. A sequence of short sentences can feel mechanical.

**Paragraphs should also have rhythm.** Complete, developed paragraphs should be the default in analytical and nonfiction writing. Short paragraphs are useful when they create emphasis, mark a meaningful turn, isolate an important observation, or improve pacing, but they should not be the basic unit of every thought.

Avoid turning every sentence into its own paragraph simply because the sentence can stand alone. Related sentences should usually be allowed to develop a complete idea together. A paragraph should generally contain enough context, explanation, evidence, or consequence to make the thought feel finished.

Good rhythm is not decoration. It helps the reader move through the argument.

## 8. Give the piece unity

Decide what kind of piece you are writing and maintain its internal logic.

Be consistent about:

- point of view
- tense
- level of formality
- terminology
- relationship with the reader
- argumentative stance

A piece can change direction during revision. If it does, follow the better direction and rewrite the earlier material to match it. Do not preserve an inconsistent structure simply because the original draft was written that way.

## 9. Give each paragraph a job

A paragraph should develop one coherent idea rather than merely provide a visual break in the page.

Related sentences should usually stay together when they explain the same idea, develop the same example, establish evidence, or draw the same implication. Break a paragraph when the thought changes, not simply because the next sentence could stand on its own.

When reviewing a draft, ask of each paragraph:

- What is this paragraph doing?
- Does it develop the paragraph before it?
- Does it prepare the reader for what follows?
- Could two adjacent short paragraphs become one stronger paragraph?
- Is the paragraph long because it contains a developed idea, or long because it contains several unrelated ideas?

The goal is not uniformly long paragraphs. The goal is **natural paragraph structure**: enough development to make ideas complete, with shorter paragraphs used deliberately for emphasis and pacing.

## 10. Earn the reader's attention

The opening sentence has a job: make the reader want the next sentence.

Avoid beginning with:

- a dictionary definition
- generic background
- obvious historical context
- broad statements about how important a topic is
- unnecessary apologies or qualifications
- meta commentary about what the article will discuss

Prefer a concrete situation, surprising observation, useful question, tension, contradiction, or story.

A strong opening should also establish enough context that the reader understands why the subject is worth following.

## 11. End when the work is done

Do not explain the conclusion after you have already made it.

Avoid repeating the thesis in slightly different language merely to make the ending feel more complete.

A strong ending can:

- resolve the central question
- reveal an implication
- leave the reader with a memorable observation
- widen the significance of the argument
- create a useful tension

Once the piece has accomplished its purpose, stop.

## 12. Use examples to make ideas visible

Abstract ideas become easier to understand when attached to something concrete.

When explaining a difficult concept, ask whether it can be shown through:

- a real situation
- a hypothetical example
- a short story
- an analogy
- a comparison
- a specific consequence

Examples should illuminate the argument, not merely decorate it.

Avoid stacking examples after the point is already clear.

## 13. Explain technical subjects like a human

Technical and scientific writing should not require the reader to already be an expert.

Start with the simplest useful concept. Establish what it is, why it matters, and what happens in practice before introducing deeper technical detail.

A useful progression is:

**simple idea → concrete example → mechanism → implications → complications**

Define specialized terms when they first become necessary. Do not assume that a technically sophisticated reader wants to work harder than necessary to understand the argument.

## 14. Be precise without becoming academic

Precision and complexity are not the same thing.

Use qualifiers when they materially improve accuracy:

- often
- sometimes
- under these conditions
- in this case
- one explanation is
- the evidence suggests

Do not use hedging as a substitute for thinking.

Avoid both extremes:

> This always happens.

and:

> It could perhaps potentially be argued that this might sometimes occur.

Say what the evidence supports and no more.

## 15. Have a point of view

Good nonfiction is not neutral merely for the appearance of neutrality.

If the evidence supports a conclusion, state it.

If an argument is weak, say why.

If you are uncertain, explain what is uncertain.

Do not hide behind passive constructions or vague references to "some people" when the writer actually has a clear position.

A point of view does not mean overstating the evidence. It means taking responsibility for the argument.

## 16. Write for one person, not an imaginary audience

Do not attempt to satisfy a fictional "general reader."

Write as if you are explaining the idea to an intelligent person who is genuinely interested but does not necessarily know what you know.

This produces more natural prose than constantly asking what "the audience" might want.

Do not flatter the reader. Do not lecture the reader. Invite the reader into the thinking.

## 17. Let curiosity drive the writing

Write about questions that are genuinely interesting.

Curiosity is visible in good nonfiction. The writer is investigating something rather than merely delivering information.

This matters especially for analytical and technical subjects. A piece should not feel like documentation unless documentation is its purpose.

Look for the question beneath the topic:

- What is surprising here?
- What doesn't fit the conventional explanation?
- What follows if this is true?
- What happens when two seemingly unrelated ideas are compared?
- What assumption deserves to be questioned?

## 18. Revise for meaning, then for language

The first draft is not the finished piece.

Revision should happen in layers.

### Pass 1: Argument

Does the piece have a clear purpose and central idea?

### Pass 2: Structure

Does each section move the reader forward? Is anything missing, misplaced, or unnecessary?

### Pass 3: Evidence

Are important claims supported? Are examples accurate and relevant? Are links provided where they strengthen credibility?

### Pass 4: Clarity

Can every paragraph be understood on the first read?

### Pass 5: Compression

What can be removed without losing meaning?

### Pass 6: Language

Replace weak verbs, vague nouns, clichés, jargon, repetition, and awkward constructions.

### Pass 7: Sound

Read the piece aloud and fix anything that does not sound like natural speech.

### Pass 8: Final judgment

Does the finished piece say what it needs to say, as clearly and strongly as possible?

## 19. Do not protect sentences you like

A sentence is not valuable because it took effort to write.

A paragraph is not valuable because it contains a clever phrase.

If a beautiful sentence does not serve the argument, remove it.

If an entire section is interesting but unnecessary, remove it.

The objective is not to preserve the draft. The objective is to improve the piece.

## 20. Use humor naturally

Humor can make serious nonfiction more engaging, especially when it comes from an honest observation.

Do not force jokes into the writing.

Avoid cleverness for its own sake. A dry observation or understated line is often more effective than an obvious punchline.

Humor should support the writer's voice rather than interrupt it.

## 21. Keep learning

Writing improves through reading, writing, revision, and attention.

Read excellent writers in the same areas you write about. Notice how they open articles, explain difficult ideas, move between paragraphs, use examples, and end arguments.

Imitation is a useful learning tool. Over time, those influences become part of a more distinct voice.

The standard is simple but demanding:

> Make each draft clearer, leaner, more precise, and more alive than the one before it.

## AI-specific application

AI-generated writing creates additional risks that these principles should explicitly guard against.

AI tends to produce prose that is fluent before it is necessarily interesting. It can also drift toward generic transitions, excessive qualification, repetitive structure, inflated language, and polished statements that lack a real point of view.

Therefore, every AI-assisted draft should be checked for:

- generic openings
- unnecessary summaries
- repetitive conclusions
- formulaic transitions
- artificial enthusiasm
- excessive use of em dashes or parenthetical asides
- vague abstractions where a concrete example would be stronger
- claims that sound authoritative but lack evidence
- unnecessary headings or list structures
- language that no human writer would naturally say
- sentences that are technically grammatical but unnecessarily complicated
- paragraphs that are fragmented into short, one- or two-sentence units without a deliberate reason
- repeated patterns of sentence length or paragraph length that make the prose feel mechanical

The goal is not to make AI writing sound "less AI." The goal is to make the writing genuinely better.

## Final checklist

Before an article is considered ready for review, ask:

- Is the central idea clear?
- Does the opening make me want to keep reading?
- Does every section earn its place?
- Does each paragraph develop a coherent idea?
- Are short paragraphs being used deliberately rather than by default?
- Can any paragraph be cut or combined with an adjacent paragraph?
- Can any sentence be shorter without losing meaning?
- Have I used the simplest accurate words?
- Have I removed clichés and jargon?
- Does the prose sound like a person?
- Have I made important claims precise and supportable?
- Are technical ideas explained clearly?
- Have I used concrete examples where abstraction becomes difficult?
- Does the piece maintain a consistent voice and point of view?
- Does the ending arrive at the right moment?
- Have I read the piece aloud?
- Is this the clearest and strongest version of the argument I can produce?
