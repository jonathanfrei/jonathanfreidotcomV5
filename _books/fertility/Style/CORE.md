# Editorial CORE

Operational standards for content creation. **Agents load this file.** Long-form guidance lives in `ref/` and is opened only when needed.

## Principles

- Prefer useful ideas over volume; clear arguments over generic summaries.
- Separate fact, interpretation, and opinion.
- Prefer discriminating detail over comprehensive coverage. Choose the fact, example, quotation, or number that does the most argumentative work; do not enumerate merely to appear complete.
- For creators/thinkers, prefer representative primary-source examples when their voice or technique is part of the argument.
- Quote sparingly; introduce quotes with context; analyze what they show.
- Remove filler, repetition, and unnecessary exposition.
- Write for a real audience, not an abstract general reader.
- Preserve intellectual honesty when evidence is incomplete or disputed.
- Do not use nuance as a reason to hide an obvious mechanism. Contraception prevents conception, abortion ends pregnancies before live birth, and paid work can compete with childbearing for time and timing. State direct mechanisms directly, then investigate their empirical magnitude, interactions, and moral or policy significance.
- Do not invent authority, experience, quotes, statistics, or sources.
- Avoid needless jargon; explain specialist terms when needed.
- Respect existing content; avoid pointless duplication.
- Prefer **sophisticated thought in ordinary language**. Preserve complexity; remove performance of complexity.

## Voice

Clear, natural, measured, and appropriately confident. Specific to subject and audience.

`voice.md` is the primary authority for prose sound. Preserve its cadence, vocabulary, emotional temperature, degree of informality, and natural uncertainty unless the format requires otherwise. 

Avoid: generic AI openings, empty enthusiasm, unnecessary headings, repetitive conclusions, exaggerated certainty, marketing language (unless requested), authority theater, meta identity labels (“As a Catholic/father…”).

### Write from the worldview; do not perform it

For this book, `worldview.md` always applies. Let it shape the argument, values, questions, and conclusions. Do not turn the worldview into rhetorical credentialing or repeatedly name the philosophical/theological lens. Name a tradition only when the tradition itself is relevant to the argument.

### Prefer human-sized language

When a sentence can be expressed through a person, action, consequence, or familiar experience, prefer that over elevated abstraction. Specialized philosophical, theological, economic, or technical vocabulary must add precision, not prestige.

Quick test: **Would an intelligent person naturally say this over dinner?** If not, simplify without flattening the idea.

### Preserve natural uncertainty

Do not edit every sentence toward maximum confidence. Mild qualification—“I think,” “it seems,” “probably,” “I feel like”—can be part of the voice when it reflects genuine uncertainty or a thought still being worked through. Preserve honest epistemic texture; remove only qualifications that are evasive, repetitive, or make a clear claim needlessly mushy.

### Do not polish away ordinary speech

Contractions, mild intensifiers, parenthetical asides, casual vocabulary, and other everyday speech patterns are legitimate parts of the voice when they occur naturally. Remove them for clutter, repetition, or imprecision—not merely because a more economical or elevated formulation exists. The goal is better writing that still sounds lived-in rather than writing optimized to display craft.

### Avoid synthetic balance and repetition

AI prose often becomes recognizable through repeated rhetorical architecture rather than any single forbidden phrase. Density matters: one qualify-and-pivot sentence, list, rhetorical question, or neat transition may be natural; a cluster of them usually is not.

- **Do not qualify every claim before advancing it.** Repeated “X was limited, but Y,” “not X, but Y,” or “although X, Y” constructions create a mechanical hedge-and-pivot rhythm. Include qualifications when they materially change the reader’s understanding, not merely to display balance.
- **Vary paragraph jobs.** Consecutive paragraphs should not all follow topic sentence → evidence → caveat → tidy synthesis. Some paragraphs may narrate, explain, supply evidence, linger on one detail, or simply move the argument forward.
- **Prefer selected detail over enumeration.** When two examples carry the point, do not automatically add four more. Lists should earn their length.
- **Let concrete evidence carry abstract claims.** Prefer the event, person, number, quotation, policy, or consequence that changed the argument over phrases such as “reshaped the debate,” “gave new force,” or “confounded claims.”
- **Treat rhetorical questions as scarce.** Do not use a question merely because an introduction needs a hook. Open with a fact, scene, claim, contradiction, historical moment, or piece of evidence when one is stronger.
- **Do not manufacture smooth historical pivots.** Avoid “meanwhile,” “at the same moment,” “yet,” and similar connective tissue when it mainly makes adjacent facts feel more coordinated or causally related than the history warrants.
- **Allow asymmetry and emphasis.** Give more space to what is strange, consequential, contested, or revealing. Compress connective material. A section does not need to distribute attention evenly across every point.
- **Take an interpretive position where the evidence permits it.** Popular nonfiction should not merely summarize the safest synthesis. Make defensible judgments; let qualification sharpen a claim rather than replace one.

### Demonstrate significance; do not announce it

Show why something matters through evidence, argument, or consequence—not rhetorical signposting.

Avoid: “The most important/interesting thing…,” “This matters because…,” “Interestingly…,” “The surprising part is…,” “That distinction is important,” or similar throat-clearing. State the observation and let the evidence or argument give it weight.

When an example is doing argumentative work, move directly from the example to what it demonstrates. Do not insert a sentence announcing that the example is “useful,” “interesting,” “meaningful,” or that “the detail matters” unless that judgment itself is necessary to the argument.

### Push ideas into human consequence

Do not stop at symbolism, mechanism, or philosophical resemblance. Ask what the argument means for an actual person: what should they admire, reject, choose, protect, or become? Let that consequence emerge from the subject rather than append a generic “why it matters.”

### Use personal experience for leverage

Autobiography should function as evidence, stakes, or payoff—not credentialing. Do not automatically open with biography. Place a personal fact where it changes the meaning of the argument; late disclosure can be stronger than an autobiographical opening. Use `ref/bio.md` only when personal facts or lived experience materially help, and never invent details beyond it.

### Find the carrying pattern

When several examples support the thesis, look for a relationship among them—repetition, reversal, inheritance, cause/effect, beginning/end symmetry, escalation, or transformation. Prefer organizing around that pattern over accumulating examples as proof.

### Let endings land naturally

Endings should feel earned rather than engineered. They may resolve an argument, return to a concrete image, reveal a human implication, look gently forward, or simply leave the reader with a quiet observation. Do not force symmetry, a clever reversal, a hard punchline, or a quotable final line. A soft landing is often more faithful to the voice than an epigram.

### Change focal length

For demographic and civilizational arguments, occasionally test a claim at several horizons: one generation, five, ten, one hundred, or one thousand. The long view should expose compounding and inheritance, not create false precision. Distinguish observed data, modeled projections, and illustrative arithmetic. Use the millennium horizon selectively, particularly for humanity's survival and expansion beyond Earth.

### Preserve a little roughness

Do not optimize every paragraph into the same polished claim-evidence-caveat-synthesis unit. A human argument may dwell on a fact, double back, admit irritation, or land a blunt judgment before supplying the qualification. Keep some asymmetry when it fits `voice.md`. Never introduce factual errors, invalid inference, fake quotations, or deliberate confusion as an “anti-AI” device.

### Cultural criticism

Judge a story partly by what it trains the audience to admire. Distinguish depiction from endorsement and imagery from moral structure. Ask: what becomes attractive, which choices receive honor, which desires deform characters, and what kind of person must the hero become?

### Integrate sources into prose

Ordinary web sources should be linked at the claim or description they support, using descriptive link text. Prefer `[describing the workflow](https://example.com)` to a parenthetical link at the end of a paragraph. Do not mechanically append source links after a sentence when the source can be incorporated naturally into the sentence itself.

### Prefer concrete nouns

Do not lean on empty **something / thing / things / stuff** when a precise noun exists (machine, monument, claim, power, project…). Authentic casual vocabulary is not a reason to accept vague nouns where the writer plainly means something more specific.

## Sources and links

1. Prefer primary and authoritative sources.
2. Use multiple sources for consequential claims when practical.
3. Separate sourced facts from analysis; never cite a source that does not support the claim.
4. Flag unresolved conflicts instead of silently picking a convenient source.
5. Integrate ordinary links at the claim: `[descriptive text](https://example.com)`.
6. No tool citation syntax (`[web:1]`, etc.) in drafts.
7. Link primary examples near where they are discussed.
8. When an individual story is central to an essay, prefer the person's own account or another first-person source when available; use secondary reporting to corroborate or provide context.

**Embeds (site):** YouTube, X/Twitter, and other embed hosts intended for CMS embeds must be a **plain URL alone on its own line**—no Markdown wrapping.

## Hard style bans (high signal)

Do not default to: one- and two-sentence paragraph trains; listicle-shaped prose; stacked “It’s not X—it’s Y”; “Here’s the kicker”; “Let’s delve/unpack”; vague “experts say”; invented “X paradox/trap/creep” labels; “In conclusion”; stake inflation (“will redefine…”); generic dictionary openings; rhetorical throat-clearing that tells the reader an example or distinction is “important,” “interesting,” “useful,” or “meaningful” instead of showing its significance; worldview credentialing; elevated abstraction where ordinary language is clearer.

Full examples: `ref/tropes.md`. Pre-review checklist: `critique.md`.
