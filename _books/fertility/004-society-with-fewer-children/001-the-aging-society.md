title: The aging society
deck: Longer lives become harder to support when too few younger people follow
eyebrow: The changing balance

Longer life is an achievement. The problem begins when long-lived generations are followed by much smaller ones. A healthy seventy-year-old may still work, travel, and watch her grandchildren. Twenty years later she may need someone else to help her stand. Low fertility changes how many people are available on either side of that exchange.
{: .lede}

The familiar population pyramid once had a wide base of children and narrowed toward old age. In a low-fertility country it becomes a column and eventually begins to turn upside down. Large generations move into retirement while smaller cohorts follow them into work.

The change is easier to see in a family. Four grandparents may have two adult children and one grandchild—the “4-2-1” shape associated with China, though the arithmetic appears anywhere one-child families persist. The grandchild will not care for six older relatives alone. Spouses, friends, paid caregivers, and public programs will share the work. The family has nevertheless become tall and narrow, with fewer siblings and cousins available when care becomes personal.

## Counting support, not birthdays

The old-age dependency ratio compares people above a conventional retirement age with people of conventional working age. Across the OECD, there were [33 people age sixty-five or older for every hundred people ages twenty to sixty-four in 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/11/rapidly-ageing-populations-will-continue-to-put-pressure-on-pension-systems.html). The organization projects 52 by 2050.

Sixty-five is an administrative line, not a biological one. A seventy-year-old running a business and caring for grandchildren is not usefully described as dependent. The [World Health Organization defines healthy aging](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/healthy-ageing-and-functional-ability) through functional ability for good reason.

Healthier aging can soften the demographic shift considerably. Across OECD countries, life expectancy at sixty-five [rose by six years between 1970 and 2023](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_8f9e3f98-en/full-report/life-expectancy-and-healthy-life-expectancy-at-older-ages_599cf359.html). If more of those years remain healthy, people can work longer and provide care rather than receive it. That is a real gain.

The difficult period comes later. WHO reports that the proportion of life spent in good health has remained broadly constant globally, so longer lives have also brought additional years of limitation. The [OECD projects](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_8f9e3f98-en/full-report/demographic-trends_257a22c9.html) Korea's share over eighty to rise from 4.4 percent in 2023 to 16.5 percent in 2050. Supporting active retirees is one problem. Providing daily help to a much larger population in its eighties and nineties is another.

Medicine may add years to life faster than it adds nurses to a ward. Low fertility makes that mismatch harder because care is labor-intensive just as the younger labor pool narrows.

Retirement ages can move as healthy life expectancy rises, and employers can make work possible for older bodies. Those changes are sensible even if fertility recovers tomorrow; the people who will be old in 2050 are already alive. What policy cannot do is retroactively enlarge the cohorts born thirty years earlier.

One generation can adjust the boundary between work and retirement. Continued contraction changes how many people stand on either side of it. Before that arithmetic reaches a pension office, though, it usually reaches a family.
