Japan counted just over [nine million vacant dwellings in 2023](https://www.stat.go.jp/english/info/news/20241030.html), 13.8 percent of its housing stock. Some were second homes or apartments waiting for tenants; others stood in neighborhoods built for households that were no longer there. The structure remained after the demand around it had changed.

Houses make the lag visible in a different way from a birth rate. Roads, water lines, shops, pension promises, and hospitals can outlast the population that once supported them, leaving a smaller generation to receive all of it at once: useful wealth, accumulated obligations, and places that may no longer have enough people nearby to work as designed.

The change is not evenly distributed. Japan's preliminary 2025 census counted [population losses in 1,558 of its 1,719 municipalities](https://www.stat.go.jp/english/info/news/20260625.html), while roughly 30 percent of the country's residents still lived in the Tokyo metropolitan area. An empty village and an expensive city apartment can be consequences of the same movement. As people and services concentrate, the places still attracting young adults can remain crowded and costly while smaller communities lose the population needed to sustain ordinary institutions.

A smaller population can remain wealthy, peaceful, and humane. Longer healthy lives can keep people working and caring for one another while scarce labor gives employers a reason to automate, and those gains can absorb a modest decline for quite a while.

Three changes are easy to confuse. A population can settle at a lower level and enjoy more land or inherited capital per person. It can pass through a period of aging as large cohorts live longer and smaller ones follow. Or it can continue producing a smaller generation each time, renewing the adjustment before the previous one is complete.

The third condition is the subject of this chapter. One smaller generation can inherit a great deal from the one before it. Repeated contraction forces people to decide again which houses, services, skills, promises, and relationships can still be carried. Some decisions arrive in a family. Others take decades to reach a government budget or research laboratory.

We begin with aging because the people who will be old over the next few decades are already here. The younger cohorts that will support them have mostly been born.
