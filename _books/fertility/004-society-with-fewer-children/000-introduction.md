Japan counted just over [nine million vacant dwellings in 2023](https://www.stat.go.jp/english/info/news/20241030.html), 13.8 percent of its housing stock. Some were second homes or apartments waiting for tenants; others stood in neighborhoods built for households that were no longer there. The structure remained after the demand around it had changed.

The book opened with empty school places in Lambeth. Houses make the same lag visible at a different scale. Roads, water lines, shops, pension promises, and hospitals can outlast the population that once supported them, leaving a smaller generation to receive all of it at once: useful wealth, accumulated obligations, and places that may no longer have enough people nearby to work as designed.

The change is not evenly distributed. Japan's preliminary 2025 census counted [population losses in 1,558 of its 1,719 municipalities](https://www.stat.go.jp/english/info/news/20260625.html), while roughly 30 percent of the country's residents still lived in the Tokyo metropolitan area. National population loss, internal movement toward Tokyo, and the concentration of jobs and services are related but not interchangeable. An empty village and an expensive city apartment can appear on the same map because one place loses residents while another receives them.

A smaller population can remain wealthy, peaceful, and humane. Longer healthy lives can keep people working and caring for one another while scarce labor gives employers a reason to automate, and those gains can absorb a modest decline for quite a while.

Several changes are easy to confuse. Low births make successor cohorts smaller. Longevity adds years of life, with changing health and care needs. International migration can alter one country's age and labor structure without enlarging the world's cohorts. Internal migration moves people toward some regions and away from others. Concentration gathers households, capital, jobs, and services into fewer places, sometimes improving efficiency while reducing access and redundancy elsewhere. A vacant house, a care shortage, or a changed age ratio may reflect more than one of these forces.

A population can settle at a lower level and enjoy more land or inherited capital per person. It can pass through a period of aging as large cohorts live longer and smaller ones follow. Or it can continue producing a smaller generation each time, renewing the adjustment before the previous one is complete. That third condition is the subject of this chapter. One smaller generation can inherit a great deal from the one before it. Repeated contraction forces people to decide again which houses, services, skills, promises, and relationships can still be carried.

No single economic number answers that question. Total output can fall while output per person rises; productivity can improve while a town loses access to care; a solvent government can lack the workers needed to fulfill its promises. The pages ahead therefore keep six measures apart: total output, output per person, productivity, fiscal capacity, access to services, and the wider civilizational capacity supplied by successors, redundancy, specialties, and room for discovery.

We begin with aging because the people who will be old over the next few decades are already here. The younger cohorts that will support them have mostly been born.
