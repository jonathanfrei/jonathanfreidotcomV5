title: What Happens When Societies Have Fewer Children?
deck: The demographic turn reaches families, work, public power, and the future a society can build
eyebrow: The consequences
---

A missing birth begins as an empty place in a family. Five years later there is one less child in a classroom. Twenty-five years later there is one less worker. By middle age, the children that person might have had are missing too. When an older parent needs help, the sibling who might have shared the week was never there.
{: .lede}

Demographic decline is unusual because the same absence returns in different forms. A smaller generation inherits the families, roads, debts, knowledge, and obligations built by larger generations before it. At first the difference can be hard to see. The school still opens, the pension check arrives, the hospital is staffed. Much of modern life was built with enough people and enough accumulated wealth to absorb a modest decline for quite a while.

That capacity is reassuring. Longer healthy lives can keep people working and caring for one another. Scarce labor gives employers a reason to automate. A smaller population can remain wealthy, peaceful, and humane. We should make every useful adaptation we can.

The harder question appears when adaptation has to be repeated. One smaller generation can inherit a great deal from the one before it. Five or ten smaller generations leave fewer people to maintain the inheritance and fewer peers with whom to divide the work. Some consequences show up first around a kitchen table. Others take decades to reach a government budget or research laboratory.

We begin with aging because the people who will be old over the next few decades are already here. The children who would have followed them are not.
