A missing birth begins as an empty place in a family. Years later it becomes a smaller school class and then one fewer worker. By the time the pension system notices, the person it expected was never there.
{: .lede}

The previous chapters traced the demographic turn and the reasons people are having fewer children. This chapter follows the consequences outward. The order is roughly chronological. The age balance changes first. Labor then grows scarce, and the families doing much of the real work of care become thinner. Governments and political power adjust after that. Environmental pressure may ease, though not automatically.

There are genuine adaptations at every step. Longer healthy lives can allow people to work more years, while scarce labor encourages automation. Smaller populations can remain wealthy and humane. Humanity’s adaptability is a reason for confidence, provided it doesn't become an excuse to ignore continued contraction.

The question is what happens when that adaptation must be repeated. A rich society can live for decades from capital and knowledge built by the proceeding larger generations. Over five or ten generations, succession becomes the central problem. Someone has to learn the difficult skill and accept work whose reward lies beyond a lifetime.

We begin with aging because that is where the arithmetic of fewer births first becomes a balance among people already alive.
