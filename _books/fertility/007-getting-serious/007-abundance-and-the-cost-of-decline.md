Many extreme proposals begin from a real constraint and answer it badly. Housing is scarce, care consumes time, careers punish interruption, and raising children competes with the income required to support them. Abundance can change which tradeoffs remain necessary. Managed decline carries costs of its own.
{: .lede}

The previous chapters developed the ordinary version of abundance: more homes near work and kin, time returned to households, medicine that heals, and institutions capable of sharing care. The more demanding point here is that a free society may have to transfer far more money, time, space, and institutional privilege toward families than current politics finds comfortable.

A family does not experience “housing supply.” It experiences the bedroom that exists, the mortgage that fits, and the grandparents who can still be reached before dinner. Creating that room may require existing owners to accept new construction, universities to shorten profitable credential paths, employers to yield control over time, and taxpayers without children to support benefits they will not receive in the same form.

Those are sacrifices in the ordinary political sense. They reallocate resources and constrain institutions while leaving people equal before the law. A childrearing pension credit can recognize that parents raise future contributors. A care income can let a household choose time over a second uninterrupted career. A large housing preference can give a growing family access to scarce space. Each remains open to argument about cost, targeting, and fairness. None requires the state to own a pregnancy.

The distinction is between sacrifice and victimization. A taxpayer may owe support to families whose work sustains shared institutions. An employer may owe parents predictable time. A professional guild may have to justify every year it keeps adults in training. A woman does not owe the state a pregnancy; an infertile man does not owe it public shame; a religious dissenter does not owe a community his entire social world as the price of leaving.

Abundance is no automatic answer. Greater wealth can support families or make solitary life easier. Technology can return time to a household or make dependence on other people seem unnecessary. Material capacity enlarges the field of choice; institutions and culture help determine what people learn to build with it.

## The bill for doing nothing

The alternatives should not be compared against an imaginary present that continues forever. Persistent fertility below replacement compounds. The first generation may enjoy cheaper housing and less competition; the next inherits more older relatives, more infrastructure per worker, and fewer siblings and cousins with whom to divide the work. Later generations make the same calculation from a smaller base.

Diffuse harms are easy to discount because no official assigns them. No ministry tells a thirty-year-old that she gets the extra night caring for an elderly aunt because there are no cousins. No cabinet announces which village school, military specialty, repair trade, or language will be the one allowed to disappear. The losses arrive through ordinary budgets and family decisions.

There are present injuries too. Adults reach the end of childbearing with fewer children than they wanted. Parents absorb work that pension systems and labor markets later depend on. A society that leaves housing scarce, work rigid, and adult formation needlessly late is not neutral merely because no official issued the loss.

Greater shared sacrifice may therefore be reasonable. The moral framework does not preserve the present distribution of money, time, and institutional power as sacred. It protects persons while allowing politics to make demanding judgments about common goods.

Some means remain wrong even against that alternative. There is no demographic emergency large enough to justify seizure, compulsory pregnancy, or eugenic production. Those acts would continue a civilization's technical capacity after corrupting its account of the human person.

Both paths in the next chapter begin with the same inherited age structure. Contraction will demand care, taxes, institutional closure, and choices about what a smaller generation can no longer carry. Renewal will demand family support, institutional change, and patience before a larger generation reaches adulthood.

Urgency changes the price of those choices. It does not change the moral status of the people who must make them. The question ahead is which sacrifices serve renewal without destroying the human freedom and dignity that make renewal worth choosing.
