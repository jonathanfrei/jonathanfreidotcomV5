Many extreme proposals begin from a real constraint and answer it badly. Housing is scarce, care consumes time, careers punish interruption, and children compete with the income required to support them. Abundance can change those tradeoffs. It cannot manufacture desire, and abundance without aspiration can coexist with atomized, low fertility.
{: .lede}

The strongest permissible program would make family life attainable: a family wage or care income, family-sized housing near work and kin, lower energy and transport costs, child-related debt and education timing, pension recognition for care, paid leave, and career seasons with protected reentry. Beneficiaries would be families and children; payers would include taxpayers, employers, current asset-holders, and institutions asked to surrender some control or revenue. Effects might appear in timing and first births before completed family size, and reversibility differs: a cash payment can be changed, a housing supply reform takes years, and a lost career season cannot simply be restored.

These policies have real burdens. Existing residents may accept construction; universities may shorten profitable credentials; employers may yield control over time; taxpayers without children may support benefits they do not receive in the same form. But the burdens are distributive and institutional, contestable through politics, and compatible with equal citizenship. They differ morally from assigning pregnancy, confiscating a body, or manufacturing a child. The test remains who pays, who benefits, who bears pregnancy and care interruptions, and whether the family can refuse another child without losing the basic support.

## The bill for doing nothing

Contraction is not a neutral baseline. Persistent fertility below replacement compounds: later generations inherit more older relatives, more infrastructure per worker, and fewer siblings and cousins with whom to divide care. Schools, repair trades, languages, military specialties, and local institutions can disappear through ordinary budgets rather than a single decree. Adults can also reach the end of childbearing with fewer children than they wanted because housing, rigid work, delayed education, and care costs made the choice practically unavailable.

Those diffuse harms do not erase the permission boundary just established. They do make passivity a choice with distributional consequences. A society may reasonably ask taxpayers to share family costs, employers to redesign time, universities to change credential paths, and cities to accept density.

Refusing every difficult reform is therefore a choice for contraction. The urgency changes the price of lawful sacrifices—the money, space, time, and institutional privilege a society is willing to reallocate—but extremity does not follow automatically. Renewal must remain worth choosing because it serves persons rather than using them.

Both paths ahead inherit the same people and age structure. Contraction will demand care, taxes, closure, and choices about what a smaller generation can carry. Renewal will demand support, institutional change, and patience before a larger generation reaches adulthood. The next chapter owns that long-horizon comparison; this one ends with the narrower conclusion that refusing all serious reform is itself a demographic decision.
