Modern education and paid work expanded women's freedom while claiming many of the same years in which families form most easily. A society trying to raise fertility could change that bargain gently. It could also decide that women should leave the labor market. The extreme proposal deserves a direct answer because the mechanism underneath it is real.
{: .lede}

“Stop hiring women” is crude enough to clear away euphemism. If employers stopped hiring women for fifteen years, more men might find work, male wages might rise, and marriage markets might again contain more men able to support a household alone. Women would face stronger pressure to marry earlier, and continuous career competition would no longer consume their twenties. Births would probably rise.

The cost is equally direct. A woman who wanted to practice medicine, leave a violent husband, support a disabled sibling, or simply use her abilities in paid work would be denied on account of sex. Employers would lose talent. Unmarried women would become dependent on relatives or the state. The policy would hand husbands bargaining power created by law rather than earned through mutual obligation. Under American law it would violate the employment protections of Title VII.

That does not make the timing conflict imaginary. The usual professional sequence asks a person to spend the late teens and twenties earning credentials, then establish a career before interrupting it. Biology supplies no comparable extension. A woman who begins trying for a first child at thirty-five may still have the family she hopes for, but she has less time to recover from infertility, pregnancy loss, or an unexpectedly long gap before a second child.

Education affects fertility through several channels. Being enrolled delays marriage and births almost mechanically. A degree can then raise earnings and the cost of leaving work. It may also change preferences and the marriage market. [Jungho Kim's review of quasi-experimental studies](https://wol.iza.org/articles/female-education-and-its-impact-on-fertility/long) finds different effects across countries and periods, so there is no honest universal coefficient. The sequence remains visible even when its precise fertility effect varies.

## What the household produces

The modern economy counts paid childcare as production and the same care by a parent as absence from the labor force. Tax rules collect revenue from market wages, then families use after-tax income to buy services that replace work once performed inside the household. This accounting can make a two-earner household look productive even when the second income barely covers childcare, commuting, and the loss of time at home.

Claudia Goldin calls the most demanding, highly rewarded positions “greedy work.” They pay a premium for long and unpredictable availability. A couple with children usually needs somebody to be available in the other direction—for a fever at school, dinner, or the ordinary hour when a child wants to talk. Two adults cannot both promise first claim on their time to an employer without buying or borrowing someone else's time at home.

One response is to make third-party care cheap enough that both parents remain continuously employed. That has real value for mothers who want or need paid work. It also embeds a preference: purchased care receives subsidy and professional continuity, while a parent providing the same hours at home absorbs lost wages, pension accrual, and status. Freedom should include the mother who wants to remain with a young child, not only the mother who wants a shorter interruption before returning to work.

The neighboring experiment is a one-earner or one-and-a-half-earner household that can actually pay rent. Family-based taxation, childrearing pension credits, and wages that do not require two uninterrupted careers could restore that option without an employment ban. Career paths could have seasons rather than one decisive tournament in the childbearing years. Fathers would also have to surrender some work and status; otherwise “supporting mothers” becomes another name for assigning women every sacrifice.

University deserves the same scrutiny. Learning is a human good. Four or six years of residential delay, debt, and credential sorting is a particular institutional package, not the definition of education. Earlier apprenticeships, commuter study, serious online instruction, and credentials earned in shorter blocks could let people learn without treating marriage and children as events scheduled after professional establishment.

G. K. Chesterton put the economic choice sharply: if the wage can be made larger, the family need not be made smaller. His family wage belonged to another economy and cannot simply be restored by wishing. The question remains. We have spent decades fitting the family to the labor market's preferred worker. A society serious about births might instead require work and education to fit the years in which people can most readily form a family.
