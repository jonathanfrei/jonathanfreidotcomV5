Modern education and paid work expanded women's freedom while claiming many of the same years in which families form most easily. A society trying to raise fertility could change that bargain gently. It could also decide that women should leave the labor market. The extreme proposal deserves a direct answer because the mechanism underneath it is real.
{: .lede}

Imagine two thirty-year-olds looking at the next five years. One has just reached the point where a career begins to compound; the other has finally finished training. They can afford rent, but not easily on one income, and childcare for two children would consume much of the second. A promotion may require travel. A move might put them three hours from grandparents. They still want the family they imagined at twenty-five. The calendar is less generous than it was then.

“Stop hiring women” is crude enough to clear away euphemism. An employment ban would try to restore one-earner households by law: reserve wages for men, make women more economically dependent on marriage, and remove continuous career competition from their twenties. It might raise births. That possibility is the reason to judge the proposal by more than its mechanism.

The cost is direct. A woman who wanted to practice medicine, leave a violent husband, support a disabled sibling, or simply use her abilities in paid work would be denied on account of sex. Employers would lose talent. Unmarried women would become dependent on relatives or the state. The policy would hand husbands bargaining power created by law rather than earned through mutual obligation. Under U.S. law it would violate Title VII's employment protections. More fundamentally, it would make a woman's equal standing conditional on a demographic theory about what other women might do.

That does not make the timing conflict imaginary. The usual professional sequence asks a person to spend the late teens and twenties earning credentials, then establish a career before interrupting it. Biology supplies no comparable extension. A woman who begins trying for a first child at thirty-five may still have the family she hopes for, but she has less time to recover from infertility, pregnancy loss, or an unexpectedly long gap before a second child.

Education affects fertility through several channels. Being enrolled delays marriage and births almost mechanically. A degree can then raise earnings and the cost of leaving work. It may also change preferences and the marriage market. [Jungho Kim's review of quasi-experimental studies](https://wol.iza.org/articles/female-education-and-its-impact-on-fertility/long) finds different effects across countries and periods, so there is no honest universal coefficient.

## What the household produces

The modern economy counts paid childcare as production and the same care by a parent as absence from the labor force. Tax rules collect revenue from market wages, then families use after-tax income to buy services that replace work once performed inside the household.

**Work the accounts miss** A mother putting a lid on a jar of preserves with a toddler on her hip, 1980. The same hours of care and household production count as absence from the labor force. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mother_and_child_canning.jpg)*
{: .caption}

The jar on the counter, the child on her hip, the meal being made while someone else is comforted: none appears as market output. Hire three people to do the same work and all of it becomes economic activity.

Claudia Goldin calls the most demanding, highly rewarded positions “greedy work.” They pay a premium for long and unpredictable availability. A couple with children usually needs somebody to be available in the other direction—for a fever at school, dinner, or the ordinary hour when a child wants to talk. Two adults cannot both promise first claim on their time to an employer without buying or borrowing someone else's time at home.

One response is to make third-party care cheap enough that both parents remain continuously employed. That has real value for mothers who want or need paid work. It also embeds a preference: purchased care receives subsidy and professional continuity, while a parent providing the same hours at home absorbs lost wages, pension accrual, and status.

The neighboring experiment is a one-earner or one-and-a-half-earner household that can actually pay rent. Family-based taxation, childrearing pension credits, a care income, and wages that do not require two uninterrupted careers could restore that option without an employment ban. Career paths could have seasons rather than one decisive tournament in the childbearing years. Protected reentry would matter as much as permission to step away.

These measures are not costless. Employers would lose some control over schedules and promotion. Universities and licensing bodies would surrender prolonged credential sequences. Taxpayers and consumers would pay more for care that households now absorb privately. Fathers would have to become less continuously available to employers and more available at home. If all of the sacrifice still lands on women, the arrangement has not solved the conflict; it has assigned it.

University deserves the same scrutiny. Learning is a human good. Four or six years of residential delay, debt, and credential sorting is a particular institutional package, not the definition of education. Earlier apprenticeships, commuter study, serious online instruction, and credentials earned in shorter blocks could let people learn without treating marriage and children as events scheduled after professional establishment.

G. K. Chesterton put the economic choice sharply: if the wage can be made larger, the family need not be made smaller. His family wage belonged to another economy and cannot simply be restored by wishing.

We have spent decades fitting the family to the labor market's preferred worker. A serious alternative may ask the labor market to yield. It may not exclude half the population from it.
