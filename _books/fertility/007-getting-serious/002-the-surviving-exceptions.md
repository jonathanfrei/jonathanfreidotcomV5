title: The surviving exceptions
deck: A few modern communities still make large families ordinary across generations
eyebrow: Evidence of magnitude
---

Some of the largest fertility differences inside rich countries appear not between benefit systems but between communities living under the same national government. Where marriage, worship, schooling, work, kinship, and family norms point in roughly the same direction, large families can remain ordinary. These communities are not policy models or controlled experiments. They are evidence about possible magnitude and about how many institutions may have to align before the difference becomes large.
{: .lede}

Israel is the national exception usually offered first. Its fertility rate has remained near three children per woman while most other wealthy countries have fallen well below replacement. The national average hides the argument, however. Israel contains several demographic worlds living beside one another.

{: .caption}

[Israeli data separated by religious observance](https://www.cbs.gov.il/en/publications/pages/2023/fertility-of-jewish-and-other-women-in-israel-by-level-of-religiosity-1979%E2%80%932022.aspx) put Haredi fertility around six and a half children per woman in the most recent reported period, religious Jewish fertility near four, and secular Jewish fertility near two. Muslim fertility, once much higher, has fallen toward three. Even the secular Jewish figure remains high beside most secular populations in Europe or East Asia.

Walk through the difference at family scale and the candidate mechanisms become easier to see. A Haredi young adult may grow up surrounded by siblings, cousins, weddings, babies, religious schools, and adults whose lives make early marriage and a large household look ordinary. The same national health system and child allowances reach a secular household living inside a different picture of adulthood.

Haredi fertility has declined from its early-2000s peak. The [Israel Democracy Institute reports](https://en.idi.org.il/haredi/2025/?chapter=63076) a fall from about 7.5 in 2003–2005 to 6.5 in 2021–2023. A decline of one birth per woman would be a demographic crisis in many countries; here it leaves fertility three times the level found in much of Europe.

## A coherent but costly ecology

The Amish provide an unusually clean American comparison. A [2025 study in *Demographic Research*](https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/52/26) estimated fertility using both American Community Survey markers and Amish population registries. The two methods converged on a total fertility rate just above six, with births concentrated in the twenties and very little childbearing outside marriage.

![Amish children walking to school, 2006](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/16/Amish_-_On_the_way_to_school_by_Gadjoboy-crop.jpg)

**An ecology, not a subsidy** Amish children on the way to school, 2006. No single payment produces a fertility rate near six. Young people encounter a dense marriage market, visible family work, and households surrounded by other large households. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Amish_-_On_the_way_to_school_by_Gadjoboy-crop.jpg)*
{: .caption}

A child walks to school past households containing several other children. Work, worship, courtship, and kinship overlap. Young people do not have to imagine what a family of five children looks like because they ate dinner in one last night. Geographic concentration keeps grandparents and cousins close; churches set expectations and supply practical help; the sacrifice of another child is interpreted among people making a similar sacrifice.

This is not an idyll. Communities with strong boundaries can impose severe costs on people who doubt, leave, or do not fit. Formal freedom to exit can coexist with the loss of family, work, language, and nearly every familiar relationship. Selection matters too: the people who remain are partly those able and willing to live within the community's demands. Fertility measured among members therefore reflects retention as well as formation.

The burdens inside the community are not evenly distributed. Women undergo pregnancy and often perform more care; men may face strong duties of provision, religious study, or conformity. Household income, education, and access to outside employment differ across the Haredi and Amish cases. A high number alone cannot tell us whether obligations are shared justly or whether dissent is treated humanely.

The Latter-day Saint experience shows that such an advantage can narrow. Church members still tend to have more children than the American average, but Utah no longer sits at the old distance from the rest of the country. In October 2025, church president Dallin H. Oaks publicly said birth rates among members had fallen significantly. The statement establishes leadership concern, not the size or cause of the change; independent demographic evidence is needed before assigning either. Delayed marriage, expensive housing, professional life, and surrounding norms can enter a religious culture rather than stopping at its boundary.

The surviving exceptions carry their force through a pattern. Worship gives family a meaning. Schools and courtship can help young adults find spouses who share it. Mutual aid makes some costs bearable, while honor makes them intelligible. Earlier marriage preserves time for several births. Selection, retention, material circumstances, and limits on exit are part of the pattern too.

A government can copy a payment. It cannot photocopy that ecology.

A demanding community entered and sustained through membership has claims on its members that a tax office does not. Even there, pressure and support can be difficult to separate. The state reaches coercion much sooner because citizens cannot ordinarily leave it without leaving their country.

The examples show that fertility near six can coexist with modern medicine and life inside a wealthy nation. They do not identify a detachable cause or a package public officials can reproduce. They leave a harder lesson: family size can differ greatly when an entire social world is organized around marriage and children, and the same coherence that supplies support can give a community formidable power over its members.
