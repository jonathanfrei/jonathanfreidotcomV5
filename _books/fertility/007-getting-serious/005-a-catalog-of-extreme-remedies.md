A thought experiment becomes evasive if it considers only ideas its author already finds respectable. The serious range begins with transferring much more wealth toward families and ends with governments claiming direct authority over marriage and reproduction. Some proposals demand a shared sacrifice while others plainly victimize a person. The same questions test the difficult middle: what mechanism produces births, how large might the effect be, who pays, whether refusal remains real, and how much power someone must hold to carry it out.
{: .lede}

## Radical support

Singapore's [2026 Marriage and Parenthood package](https://www.population.gov.sg/marriage-parenthood-measures-at-national-day-rally-2026/) sits at the supportive edge. The announced package provides up to S$62,000 for each citizen child through a cash gift, annual credits, development and education accounts, alongside expanded leave, preschool support, and an additional public-housing ballot chance for each child. Much of it begins in 2027 or later. It is a prospective policy illustration, not evidence of a fertility effect.

Now make the transfer impossible to miss. A child could cancel student debt, qualify a family for an apartment, or unlock an account worth a substantial share of a year's pay. Government could cover birth and medical costs while sending a parent an income for care. At some size the benefit stops smoothing a household budget and begins changing the adult life available to the recipient.

A parent might see the deposit arrive before leaving the hospital. A childless worker might see the other side every two weeks on a pay stub.

## Tax, privilege, and public rank

Governments have taxed bachelors and childless adults before. A modern childless surtax could finance pensions whose future workers are being raised by somebody else. Designed one way, it makes dependents count more honestly. Designed another, humiliation is part of the mechanism.

Parent priority could extend beyond taxes into housing queues, public appointments, or political representation. The likely fertility effect of a small privilege would be slight. The equal-citizenship effect could be immediate. A parent may deserve compensation for costs borne on behalf of a future generation without acquiring greater civic worth than a neighbor.

Benefits could also concentrate on third and fourth children. Persuading a couple with two children to have another may require less cultural movement than persuading an unmarried adult to form a family from the beginning. [Germany credits up to three years of childrearing per child](https://www.deutsche-rentenversicherung.de/DRV/DE/Rente/Familie-und-Kinder/Kindererziehung/kindererziehung_node.html) toward the public pension of the parent who primarily provided the care. A much larger credit would make the pension system acknowledge that raising its future contributors is productive work.

## Reordering adult institutions

Law could shorten credential requirements, support apprenticeships, remove marriage penalties, protect reentry after care, or limit geographic transfers that separate kin. These measures alter institutions around choice. Reserving jobs by sex, making benefits depend on marriage, or operating a national matchmaking file alters who may choose and on what terms. Each step gives an administrator more authority over adult life; the relevant question is whether that authority removes an artificial barrier or assigns a person a social role.

## Conception, pregnancy, and parenthood

The [earlier practice-by-practice account](/books/fertility/why-fewer-children/reproductive-control) also enters the catalog when institutions fund, regulate, restrict, or compel those practices. The question now is not what contraception, abortion, sterilization, or assisted reproduction does. It is what authority a government, employer, clinic, or community may claim over conception, pregnancy, embryonic life, and parenthood. Protecting a life already begun is not reproductive conscription; demanding a future conception is not protection. Restorative medicine is not embryo production. The next section tests these differences through power, proportionality, and the possibility of refusal.

## Direct command and production

A state could assign spouses, impose reproductive quotas, confine people during their fertile years, or punish failure to produce a child. China's one-child policy showed that a quota can reach from a national plan into a pregnancy. Reversing the number on the quota would preserve the same claim of ownership. Kidnapping and sexual violence are the oldest versions, with no administrative euphemism left.

Technology opens another corridor. In-vitro gametogenesis aims to create eggs or sperm from other cells; artificial-gestation research has extended the survival of extremely premature animals; embryo selection and cloning make reproduction increasingly separable from sexual union and pregnancy. None currently supplies a scalable human-birth program. The relevant speculation is institutional, not predictive: a government or corporation could seek authority to produce embryos, select traits, assign parenthood, and give children a planned social purpose. Intimate data and artificial intelligence could turn matchmaking into prediction, then pressure, then assignment.

The catalog is deliberately uncomfortable, though shock is not an argument. Some proposals transfer money or institutional power; others alter equal citizenship; others claim bodies, marriages, pregnancies, embryos, or children. The sorting begins with what each proposal asks one person to do—or permits another person to do to her—and whether the good invoked can ever authorize that act.
