A thought experiment becomes evasive if it considers only ideas its author already finds respectable. The serious range begins with transferring much more wealth toward families and ends with governments claiming direct authority over marriage and reproduction. The same questions apply across it: what mechanism produces births, how large might the effect be, who pays, and how much power must someone hold to carry it out?
{: .lede}

Singapore's [2026 Marriage and Parenthood package](https://www.population.gov.sg/marriage-parenthood-measures-at-national-day-rally-2026/) sits at the respectable edge. Its new support can reach S$62,000 per citizen child, with cash in the first year, annual payments through age sixteen, education accounts, more leave, and an extra public-housing ballot chance for each child. The baseline is the same at every birth order. This is “make parenthood easier” carried out with unusual administrative competence and considerable money. It is too new to have demonstrated a fertility recovery.

A more radical version would shift enough income and housing toward parents to make the transfer impossible to miss. A child could cancel student debt, qualify a family for an apartment, or unlock an account worth a substantial share of a year's pay. Government could cover birth and medical costs while sending a parent an income for care. At some size the benefit stops smoothing a household budget and begins changing the adult life available to the recipient. That is the point.

The distributional conflict would also become plain. Adults without children would finance families with children, poor parents might receive less than wealthy parents through tax deductions, and people unable to conceive could pay for benefits conditioned on a status they cannot attain. Generosity does not remove power. It makes the transfer large enough that the public must defend its purpose.

## Sticks and privileges

The same transfer can be turned around. Governments have taxed bachelors and childless adults before. A modern childless surtax could appear on every paycheck and finance pensions whose future workers are being raised by somebody else. It might make dependents count more honestly. If designed to brand the childless as free riders, humiliation would be part of the mechanism rather than an unfortunate side effect.

Parent priority could extend beyond taxes. Parents might receive faster permit processing, preferred parking, housing queues, or extra votes attached to the children they represent. A parent-only franchise would align political power with people invested in the next generation, according to its defenders. It would also remove equal citizenship from an adult because of family status. The likely fertility effect seems small; the constitutional effect would be immediate.

Another approach would concentrate benefits on third and fourth children. Persuading a couple with two children to have another may require less cultural movement than persuading an unmarried adult to form a family from the beginning. Pension credits already recognize part of this logic. [Germany credits up to three years of childrearing per child](https://www.deutsche-rentenversicherung.de/DRV/DE/Rente/Familie-und-Kinder/Kindererziehung/kindererziehung_node.html) toward the public pension of the parent who primarily provided the care. A much larger credit could make the pension system acknowledge that raising its future contributors is productive work.

## Reordering adult life

Law could shorten credential requirements, support apprenticeships, and remove marriage penalties. It could go further: reserve jobs by sex, require national service organized around courtship, limit geographic transfers that separate kin, or operate a national matchmaking service. Cities could plan new settlements around large homes and nearby grandparents. These proposals address different breaks in the path to family, though each asks an administrator to decide what adult life should look like.

Practices that directly prevent births offer a more immediate mechanism. Abortion ends a pregnancy before live birth, and the evidence reviewed earlier shows that abortion law changes births. Contraception prevents conception. Governments can protect unborn life, stop subsidizing contraception, restrict sterilization, make divorce harder, or regulate reproductive medicine. Enforcement would fall unevenly and could create black markets. The moral case for protecting an existing child is also different from using a woman's possible future conception to meet a population target.

The boundary cases remove all indirection. A state could assign spouses, impose reproductive quotas, confine people during their fertile years, or punish failure to produce a child. China's one-child policy showed that a quota can reach from a national plan into a pregnancy. Reversing the number on the quota would preserve the same claim of ownership. Kidnapping and sexual violence are the oldest versions, with no administrative euphemism left.

Technology opens another direction. In-vitro gametogenesis may someday create eggs or sperm from other cells; artificial-womb research may extend the survival of extremely premature infants; embryo selection and cloning make reproduction increasingly separable from sexual union and pregnancy. None currently supplies a scalable human-birth program. It is still possible to imagine a government or corporation producing embryos, selecting traits, and gestating children to order. Intimate data and artificial intelligence could meanwhile turn matchmaking into prediction, then pressure, then assignment.

The catalog is deliberately uncomfortable. Some items might recover tenths of a birth, others could plausibly move family size by whole children, and the most direct methods would work fastest by giving people the least room to refuse. Effectiveness does not rise in a neat line with cruelty, but administrative control often does. The sorting has to begin with what each proposal asks one person to do—or permits someone else to do to her—rather than with the elegance of its demographic model.
