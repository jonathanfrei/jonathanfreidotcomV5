A rocket can carry explorers, and an outpost can persist on cargo and instructions from Earth. A home begins when residents build institutions they expect to remain after the founding crew is gone. The work then belongs to generations: people who inherit a place, repair it, question it, and decide whether its horizon should widen.
{: .lede}

That is why the stars belong in a book about fertility. Machines can multiply what people can do. They can survey, fabricate, diagnose, and carry knowledge into dangerous places. They cannot be successors in the human sense. They do not receive a promise, raise a child, keep a faith, revise a law, or decide that a founding plan has become unjust. Those tasks remain with persons and the institutions they form.

## Work no founder can finish

For one generation, renewal supplies apprentices to projects already underway. Across several, it gives schools, workshops, courts, families, and settlements successors after the founders are gone. At the scale of centuries, it may determine whether humanity remains a visitor beyond Earth or learns to live there.

No generation can demand that its descendants complete a particular project. Free people should be able to abandon a bad settlement, change its purpose, or turn their attention homeward. Keeping capacity alive does not dictate its use. It prevents incapacity from making the decision first.

The nearer work is ordinary: receiving a child, keeping a promise, teaching an apprentice, maintaining a water system, planting trees whose shade belongs to someone else. These acts do not look like interstellar civilization, but they contain its grammar. They teach people to receive an inheritance without treating it as property and to continue work whose final form they will never see.

The final movement returns to the decisions close at hand. The distant horizon does not settle what a couple should choose, what a town owes its children, or what public authority may ask of a family. It only makes the inheritance visible: a civilization remains capable of reaching farther when it is willing to welcome, form, repair, and hand on life here.

Whether humanity keeps that horizon open will not be decided by a single launch. It will be decided over generations by the kind of people and institutions we become. The work is unfinished, which is another way of saying that it is still ours to receive.
