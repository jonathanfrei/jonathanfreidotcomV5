Future generations cannot consent to the world we leave them. They will inherit its climate, debts, laws, and technical capacity, along with either a civilization that kept open the path beyond Earth or one that slowly spent the margin required to attempt it.
{: .lede}

UNESCO's [1997 declaration on future generations](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/declaration-responsibilities-present-generations-towards-future-generations) joins the maintenance of humankind to respect for human dignity. The pairing is right: continuity purchased by conscripting present people into pregnancy or parenthood would betray the civilization being continued.

The off-world case makes the danger especially clear. A very small settlement may know exactly how many births would improve its age structure or genetic resilience, but that knowledge gives its leaders no authority to assign pregnancies, compel conception, or treat a woman as a reproductive asset owed to mission control.

That limit should not be confused with a requirement that the settlement remain morally neutral about every practice affecting reproduction. A community can forbid coercive pregnancy while still making legal judgments about abortion, contraception, public subsidy, and the duties owed to unborn life. Refusing to force a person to conceive is not the same claim as promising access to every means of preventing conception or ending a pregnancy.

Small scale does make freedom harder. When one person's pregnancy, occupation, or refusal can materially affect everyone else, planners will be tempted to turn necessity into authority. The best defense is margin: enough people, capacity, and competing institutions that one person's choice does not threaten the whole settlement. Scale can protect liberty because it allows some inefficiency, dissent, and exit.

## What we owe forward

A durable settlement should offer more than biological survival. Future people should inherit room to pursue goods the founders did not choose for them; a technically alive colony with one permitted occupation and no meaningful way to leave has preserved bodies while shrinking human life.

That obligation runs backward too. Earth is a home to repair rather than raw material for escape. Expansion should carry the best of this inheritance outward while making us more careful with the original. Technologies built for closed environments may help, but the deeper discipline is the same: receive a place you did not make and leave it habitable for people you will never meet.

Long projects can become vanity when leaders spend other people's money on monuments to themselves. Space exploration deserves ordinary scrutiny over cost, risk, and power. It becomes easier to justify when the capabilities it develops also improve life nearer at hand and when the people paying for it remain free to change course.

The moral claim is modest. We do not owe descendants Mars. We owe them, as far as we reasonably can, a civilization still capable of choosing it.

Even that choice remains subordinate to nearer duties. A civilization that reaches another planet while abandoning the poor, treating embryos as material, or exhausting the home it already received has expanded its range without enlarging its humanity. Technical survival is not the final measure of a human future. The point of keeping possibilities open is to leave future people room to seek truth, love one another, worship, build families, and pursue goods no machine or population total can contain.
