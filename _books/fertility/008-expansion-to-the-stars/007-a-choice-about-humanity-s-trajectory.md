A growing population supplies one foundation for a settlement on Mars. The builders will also need technical skill and the patience to work for strangers in the future. Sustained contraction weakens each attempt by leaving fewer successors and a smaller margin after maintenance.
{: .lede}

This is a claim about direction rather than a launch date. Humanity could grow for centuries without moving beyond expeditions. A smaller population might achieve a brilliant burst of exploration through automation. The question is what happens when the burst must become an inheritance.

The steps described in this chapter are severe. We have to keep people alive far from Earth's protective environment and build an economy that can recover from broken links with home. Raising children safely there is a further problem with very little human evidence. There is plenty here to humble even an optimist.

## The work beneath the symbol

The stars can become a cheap symbol of confidence, a way to make ordinary family life sound grander than it feels. Most parents will never think about Mars while waking with a baby at three in the morning. Their care is still part of the same generational work. They are raising the people who will decide which projects deserve to continue.

Family formation and space expansion meet in that succession. A government program assigning children to a mission would corrupt both. A civilization makes room for marriage and parenthood because those are human goods near at hand. Over time, the renewed generations preserve the possibility of larger undertakings. The order matters.

The space project can return the favor by changing what the culture admires. It shows young people a future with difficult work left to do. It gives engineering and sacrifice a visible object beyond consumption. The story becomes false if the rockets are impressive while the society beneath them cannot form homes. A permanent settlement is finally a family story.

## An open horizon

For one generation, demographic renewal supplies apprentices to projects already underway. Across five, it can sustain institutions whose founders are gone. At the scale of a millennium, it may separate a civilization confined to one vulnerable world from a human presence spread across the solar system. The arithmetic is illustrative; the inheritance is not.

We may discover that some destinations are inhospitable or that living there is not worth the cost. Free people should be able to turn back from a bad project. Preserving capacity does not dictate its use. It prevents incapacity from making the decision for them.

That is the choice beneath the fertility decline. We can adapt well enough to contraction that its comforts conceal the narrowing horizon. Or we can use the time a slow-moving danger gives us to make family life possible again and preserve a civilization with room to grow.

The first path may remain pleasant for a long while. The second is harder to picture because it contains people and achievements no forecast can name. I think we should leave them the larger future.
