A rocket can carry explorers. A base can survive for years on cargo and instructions from Earth. A civilization begins when people expect the place to remain after they are gone and raise children who inherit it.
{: .lede}

That is the thread running through this chapter. Expansion requires technical skill and money, but those are not enough. The work has to pass from one generation to another until the settlement no longer belongs mainly to the people who began it.

This may take centuries. We still have to learn how to keep people healthy far from Earth's protective environment, build supply chains that can survive broken links with home, and understand whether children can develop safely in altered gravity. There is plenty here to humble an optimist.

## The work beneath the symbol

The stars can become an easy symbol of confidence, which is exactly why they need to stay connected to ordinary life. Most parents will never think about Mars while waking with a baby at three in the morning. They are still doing the generational work on which every long project depends.

A permanent settlement makes the connection harder to ignore. The child born there is not a symbol. She needs adults who love her more than they love the mission, and a society large enough that her future is not determined by the spreadsheet that justified her birth. If she grows up and chooses a different vocation from the one planners expected, that is evidence the settlement has become more human, not less efficient.

Space exploration can return something to the culture that supports it. It gives young people a visible reminder that difficult work remains unfinished and that some projects are worth beginning without expecting to see the end. The story becomes false if the rockets are impressive while the society underneath them cannot form homes or sustain the people who will inherit the work.

## An open horizon

For one generation, demographic renewal supplies apprentices to projects already underway. Across several, it gives institutions successors after the founders are gone. At the scale of centuries, it may determine whether humanity remains a visitor beyond Earth or learns to live there.

We may discover that some destinations are inhospitable or simply not worth the cost. Free people should be able to abandon a bad project. Preserving capacity does not dictate its use. It prevents incapacity from making the decision first.

That is why the space argument belongs in a book about fertility. The point is not that people should have children so somebody can colonize Mars. Marriage, parenthood, and family are goods much nearer at hand. Their renewal also keeps larger possibilities open for people whose names we will never know.

Before humanity can build a home on another world, it has to recover confidence in building families on this one. The final question is therefore closer to home: whether we choose to grow again, and what kind of life would make that choice seem worth making.
