title: From exploration to settlement
deck: A place becomes a human home when it can sustain childhood, dependence, and succession

The [International Space Station has been continuously inhabited for twenty-five years](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/25-years-of-space-station-technology-driving-exploration/), and it remains an outpost. Its residents arrive as selected adults. Cargo vehicles bring provisions and replacement parts, while mission control surrounds every crew with expertise on Earth. A settlement begins when absence from Earth becomes survivable.
{: .lede}

The distinction appears in the water. NASA recently demonstrated [98 percent water recovery aboard the station](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/nasa-achieves-water-recovery-milestone-on-international-space-station/). Two percent sounds trivial until there is no resupply. The machinery also needs filters, replacement parts, and people who know when its readings are lying. Closing a loop means keeping it closed after the specialist who designed it is gone.

Demography works on a similar timescale. A settlement can look almost independent while importing a small number of people from Earth every year. Over one generation that may be enough. Over several, the question becomes whether the population can replace itself without a continuing demographic supply line.

## The first child changes the project

An expedition is organized around healthy adults selected for danger. A town has to make room for people who are dependent, inconvenient, or simply ordinary. Human reproduction beyond Earth is largely unstudied. NASA's current research still relies heavily on animal and cellular experiments to understand how altered gravity affects development, while radiation outside Earth's magnetic field adds another concern.

Until children can be conceived, born, and raised safely, an off-world population remains a rotating workforce supplied by Earth. That is not a failure. It is simply a different category from settlement.

The first permanent child changes the obligations of everyone around her. She needs adults who think beyond the next launch window. She eventually needs peers, teachers, law, and some possibility of leaving a bad situation without leaving the habitable world. She did not volunteer for the founders' risk tolerance or sign the charter that governs the base.

## How many is enough

Researchers have not agreed on a minimum population for a self-sustaining settlement. Genetic models produce very different answers because they assume different voyage lengths and reproductive rules. Cameron Smith's review of multigenerational interstellar travel proposed a founding population in the [rough range of 14,000 to 44,000](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2013.12.013), while other models have produced lower estimates under tighter assumptions. None is a demonstrated plan for a real colony.

The disagreement is useful mainly because genetics is only one floor. A population could avoid inbreeding and still be too small to sustain enough specialties, absorb an accident, or let people escape a coercive community. Scale buys redundancy. It can also buy freedom.

Governance will grow from practical control. Whoever allocates oxygen, medical care, and return seats possesses unusual power. Mission command may be necessary during construction. Children born under it cannot remain permanent crew. A settlement becomes a polity when the people living there can inherit its rules and change them.

A visitor looks back at Earth. A settler buries a parent and teaches a child the names of places neither of them intends to leave. Technology makes that scene possible. Succession makes it a civilization.
