Managed decline is not costless. It can mean closed towns, thinner families, abandoned institutions, heavier care burdens, lost knowledge, weaker states, and a civilization progressively less able to choose another direction. Taking those losses seriously does not mean that anything capable of preventing them becomes acceptable.
{: .lede}

## The comparison to make

- Distinguish temporary hardship from permanent generational contraction.
- Identify losses that adaptation can absorb and losses that become irreversible.
- Compare concentrated harms imposed by a remedy with diffuse harms imposed by decline.
- Ask who bears each cost, who consents, who can exit, and who is never represented in the decision.
- Separate politically unpopular sacrifice from violations of human dignity.
- Consider whether failure to act can itself become an injustice toward children, the poor, the elderly, or future generations.

## Questions at the edge

- How much wealth, leisure, autonomy, or inherited entitlement should a society surrender to make family life possible?
- When does taxation for family support become unjust, and when does refusing support simply hide costs inside households?
- Could severe limits on consumption or geographic mobility be preferable to demographic contraction?
- Which institutions are worth preserving, and which should be allowed to disappear rather than impose an immoral remedy?
- Can a civilization be lost through demographic passivity?
- Can it also be lost by surviving through means that corrupt its understanding of the person?

## Intended conclusion

The chapter should not end with an extreme program. It should leave the reader with a stricter standard for seriousness. Decline has real victims and may demand sacrifices larger than contemporary politics admits. Some measures can be reconsidered under conditions of abundance or voluntary association. Other measures remain wrong even if they work. The task is to become capable of more than managed decline without making continuity an excuse for domination.
