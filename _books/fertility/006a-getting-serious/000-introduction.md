There is a point in every slow crisis when the scale of the remedy begins to look smaller than the scale of the problem. Fertility policy has mostly remained on the comfortable side of that line: another tax credit, another month of leave, another childcare subsidy. This chapter will cross the line in thought, not recommendation, and ask what societies have tried, what they might conceivably try, and what no fear of decline could make permissible.
{: .lede}

## Purpose of the chapter

This chapter is a bounded thought experiment. Inclusion will not imply endorsement, legality, morality, feasibility, or likely effectiveness. Some measures belong to myth, some to authoritarian history, some to functioning contemporary communities, and some only to speculative futures. They should not be flattened into one category.

The object is to widen the field of inquiry without losing the person inside it. A civilization may have good reason to resist permanent contraction. It still cannot make women, men, children, embryos, or families into material for a demographic plan.

## Questions to resolve

- When do ordinary family policies become visibly inadequate to the trajectory?
- What can historical cases reveal without turning cruelty into a policy precedent?
- Which high-fertility societies reproduce themselves through conviction, institution, pressure, selection, subsidy, or some combination?
- Which measures are merely unpopular, which are unrealistic, and which violate limits that consequences cannot erase?
- Can an abundant future recover the useful mechanism inside a bad proposal without preserving its coercion or injustice?
- What losses caused by managed decline are serious enough to justify sacrifice, and which sacrifices would destroy the civilization being preserved?
