The human population is still growing. Beneath that total, something has changed: in much of the world, each generation is smaller than the one before. The difference appears slowly—an empty desk, a missing worker, an aging parent with fewer children to call.
{: .lede}

For two centuries, the great population fear was that humanity would become too numerous. We may be entering the opposite problem before we have fully noticed the turn. A society can become smaller once and remain capable. When every generation is smaller, the adjustment begins again from a narrower base.

This book follows that change from a school with too few pupils to the possibility that people might one day build a home beyond Earth. It asks why people are having fewer children, what the decline changes, what governments and institutions can do, and whether renewal can remain compatible with human dignity and modern freedom.

The first chapter begins where demographic change usually becomes visible: not in a distant forecast, but in a building that expected another class to arrive.

## Author's note

The conversation about falling fertility on X drew my attention to the subject and sent me down a research rabbit hole. What began as an attempt to understand a few surprising charts became a much larger question about families, institutions, and what one generation leaves for the next.

I have five children, so the subject isn't abstract to me. I know the noise and work of family life, along with the strange experience of watching a new person slowly become herself. I want other people to see the generational stakes too—not because every life should follow the same pattern, but because a future with fewer and fewer successors changes what all of us can receive, sustain, and hand forward.

This is an AI-enabled project. I directed and used AI throughout the research, organization, drafting, checking, and revision of the book. The judgments, arguments, and final editorial decisions are mine. Sources are linked throughout, and the web edition will continue to be corrected and updated as the evidence changes and readers find things I missed.

You can enter through any chapter above, but the argument begins with a slower and more ordinary sign of change: a few empty places in a classroom.
