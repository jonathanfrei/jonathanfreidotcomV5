South Korea recorded a total fertility rate of 0.75 in 2024. The number is so far below replacement that even its meaning can be hard to feel. It describes one year's childbearing pattern, not a permanent national fate, but years near that level have already made the youngest cohorts far smaller than the generations ahead of them.
{: .lede}

The 2024 result was an improvement. TFR rose from 0.72, and births increased for the first time in nine years. That is genuinely encouraging. It also shows why a rate and a count must be read together: a recovery now works through a smaller number of potential parents than the country had before the long decline.

Japan reached this stage earlier. Its population fell by 550,000 in the year ending October 2024, the fourteenth consecutive annual decline. Long life contributes to the shape in the best sense: more people survive into old age. After decades of low fertility, however, numerous older cohorts eventually produce more deaths while smaller younger cohorts produce fewer births.

China brings the same turn to a different scale and history. The one-child policy accelerated fertility decline through coercion and left injuries that cannot be reduced to a population chart. Ending the policy did not restore large families. In 2024, mainland China recorded 9.54 million births and 10.93 million deaths; its population fell by 1.39 million even though births rose from the previous year.

Hong Kong and Taiwan have also recorded fertility near the lowest levels in the world. Singapore's resident TFR was 0.97 in 2024 after decades of family policy. These places differ in government, housing, migration, and history. Their common result does not prove one cause. It makes simple explanations harder to sustain.

## The range of near-term outcomes narrows

Later chapters will examine late marriage, housing, work hours, educational competition, gender expectations, contraception, and the unusually close relationship between marriage and childbearing in much of the region. Chapter 2 needs only the demographic consequence.

Policy can help today's adults marry or have the children they want. It cannot quickly create the adults who would be forming families now if more children had been born twenty-five years ago. Immigration could widen the pool, though East Asian countries have generally used it less than North American ones and it would bring its own political and institutional choices.

This is negative momentum before the mathematics is fully visible. Low fertility first changes the maternity ward. A generation later, it changes how many people can enter it as parents. An annual increase can begin a recovery, but the age structure determines how quickly that recovery reaches the population as a whole.
