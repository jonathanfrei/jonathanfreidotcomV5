In 1968, Paul and Anne Ehrlich published *The Population Bomb* into a world that had good reason to be frightened by population growth. The number of people alive had doubled in less than fifty years, and the annual growth rate was near its historical peak. The Ehrlichs predicted mass starvation. Governments worried about too many births. Population control became a serious part of international development policy.
{: .lede}

The population kept growing. The explosion did not.

That reversal is the subject of this chapter. Humanity did not escape the old population problem because famine restored a Malthusian balance. Food production rose, children stopped dying at extraordinary rates, and families eventually began having fewer children. The same demographic transition that produced the great surge in population also contained the mechanism that would slow it.

We are living in the long overlap between those two eras. The world still adds people because earlier generations were large and people live longer. At the same time, more than half of countries and areas are already below replacement fertility. Some countries are shrinking. Others will grow for decades even though their families have become much smaller. The global total hides all of this movement.

The history helps explain the confusion. For two centuries, population growth looked like the durable problem and scarcity like the natural consequence. Then fertility fell faster and further than the older models expected. Europe showed that low fertility could persist. East Asia pushed it to levels that once would have seemed implausible. Countries in Latin America and Asia compressed into a few decades a transition that took much longer in Europe.

The mechanism is simple enough to describe and slow enough to overlook. Death rates fall first. Birth rates follow. Population surges in between. If fertility then remains below replacement, the large generations eventually give way to smaller ones. For a while, momentum keeps the population growing anyway. Later, the same momentum can work in reverse.

That delay is the part worth understanding. By the time decline appears in the headline population number, the smaller generation that will shape the next few decades has already been born. Or it hasn't.
