A fertility rate compresses millions of births into one decimal. For the regional comparisons ahead, four ideas do most of the work.
{: .lede}

The total fertility rate, or TFR, describes births occurring now. It combines the age-specific birth rates in a particular year to estimate how many children a hypothetical woman would have if those rates continued throughout her reproductive life; a TFR of 1.5 does not mean the average woman alive has 1.5 children.

![South Korea total fertility rate, 1925 to 2020](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/39/Total_Fertility_Rate_%28TFR%29_in_Korea.svg/960px-Total_Fertility_Rate_%28TFR%29_in_Korea.svg.png)

**A period rate, not a completed family** South Korea's total fertility rate from 1925 to 2020. A TFR of 0.84 in 2020 describes that year's age-specific birth rates, not how many children the average woman then alive had. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Total_Fertility_Rate_(TFR)_in_Korea.svg)*
{: .caption}

Replacement fertility is the level at which one generation replaces itself in the long run—roughly 2.1 children per woman in low-mortality countries. Staying below that level does not produce population decline immediately because a country with many young adults can keep growing for years while a large number of women enter childbearing age.

Timing can also move the annual number. If women postpone births, TFR can fall even when some of those births occur later, so South Korea's increase from 0.72 in 2023 to 0.75 in 2024 is worth noticing without calling it a reversal. Completed cohort fertility, measured after a generation has largely finished having children, tells us more about lifetime family size but arrives much later.

Finally, migration changes national population without changing the global number. A low-fertility country can keep growing by attracting young adults from elsewhere, which may be economically valuable and can alter its age structure quickly. It does not create additional people at the world level.

Those distinctions are enough for what follows. Fertility tells us how intensely people are having children now. Age structure tells us how many potential parents there are. Migration can change the national result. Read together, they explain why countries with similarly small families can have very different population trajectories.
