---
title: The end of the population explosion
deck: A rising total can conceal a falling growth rate and shrinking generations
eyebrow: The great reversal
---

For most of human history, the number of people alive changed slowly. Then it did something extraordinary. Humanity reached one billion people in the early nineteenth century, two billion in 1927, and eight billion in 2022. What looked like the permanent direction of modern life was one passage through a demographic transition.

The distinction between a level and a rate explains much of the confusion around population now. A car can keep moving forward while slowing down. In the same way, world population can add people each year even as the annual rate of growth falls. Growth ends only when the number added reaches zero. Decline begins when deaths exceed births and migration does not offset the difference.

At the global level, migration cancels out: humanity does not gain a person when someone crosses a border. At the national level, it can determine whether a country grows or shrinks. Births, deaths, and migration therefore need to remain separate even when they combine into one headline population total.

The population explosion began with a human victory. More children survived and adults lived longer while birth rates remained high for a time. The distance between births and deaths widened. The world's annual population growth rate reached its high point in the 1960s, but the total continued rising because large young generations were entering adulthood and because people were living longer.

That inherited age structure still matters. The United Nations estimated 8.2 billion people in 2024 and projects a peak near 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s under its central scenario. The same revision reports that 63 countries and areas, containing more than a quarter of the world's population, had already passed their population peak by 2024. A global total can rise while China, Japan, and parts of Europe contract.

Japan makes the transition visible. Its postwar birth cohorts were much larger than the cohorts born near the end of the twentieth century. Births fell below deaths in the late 2000s, and the gap widened. By October 2024, Japan's population had declined for fourteen consecutive years.

![Live births and deaths in Japan, 1946 to 2019](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Live_births_and_deaths_of_Japan_%281946-2019%29.png/960px-Live_births_and_deaths_of_Japan_%281946-2019%29.png)

**When deaths overtake births** Live births and deaths in Japan, 1946–2019. The two lines cross only after decades of change in fertility, survival, and age structure. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Live_births_and_deaths_of_Japan_(1946-2019).png)*
{: .caption}

This does not fix every country to Japan's path. Fertility can rise, mortality can change, and migration can transform national trajectories. It does establish a different starting point. The old default question—how will the world absorb another billion people?—now sits beside another one: what happens when more countries stop replacing one generation with the next?

To see why the older question held such power, it helps to return to the argument that population would repeatedly press against a fixed supply of food.
