title: Contraception, abortion, and reproductive technology
deck: Modern medicine made births easier to prevent than to recover after years of delay
eyebrow: Controlling reproduction
---

Modern reproductive technology changed fertility in two opposite directions: contraception and abortion made births easier to prevent, while IVF and related treatments later made some otherwise impossible births possible. The asymmetry is the important part. Postponing or preventing a birth is usually easier than creating one after age has reduced fertility.
{: .lede}

Reliable contraception separated sex from childbearing more completely than earlier generations could have imagined. In 2022–2023, [54.3 percent of American females ages fifteen to forty-nine](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db539.htm) were currently using contraception; globally, [77.6 percent of women who wanted to avoid pregnancy](https://unstats.un.org/sdgs/report/2024/Goal-03/) had that need satisfied by a modern method in 2024.

Couples can have serious reasons to postpone another pregnancy: illness, financial instability, exhaustion, or obligations to children already born. The reason and the means are separate moral questions. Periodic abstinence asks a couple to govern sexual desire around the body's fertile rhythms. Contraception changes the act itself so that sex can continue while its procreative capacity is deliberately blocked. [*Humanae Vitae* makes this distinction in its account of responsible parenthood](https://www.vatican.va/content/paul-vi/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_p-vi_enc_25071968_humanae-vitae.html#_The_Observance_of), allowing spouses to avoid a birth for serious reasons while rejecting acts intended to make conception impossible.

The difference reaches beyond one technique. Once reliable contraception becomes the default setting of adult sexual life, pregnancy becomes something that normally happens only after an affirmative decision to stop preventing it. A couple can spend years in a sexual relationship while postponing parenthood with high confidence; waiting no longer requires abstinence or much risk of an unintended child forcing the timetable forward.

This is how contraception connects to the rest of the chapter. It does not merely reduce unplanned births; it makes every other reason for delay easier to act on. The couple can wait for the wedding, the house, the promotion, or the feeling of readiness while a contraceptive routine continues almost automatically and the decision to have a child requires a deliberate reversal.

The political question is separate from the demographic mechanism, but it is not morally empty. A government can reject coercive population policy without treating contraception as a positive obligation of the state. Law and subsidy teach as well as distribute. When public policy treats the deliberate exclusion of fertility from sex as ordinary healthcare, it reinforces the expectation that pregnancy is a preventable failure until explicitly chosen. Demography can show the resulting change in births. Moral judgment has to ask what view of sex, marriage, and human dependence the policy makes normal.

## Abortion and births

Abortion affects fertility more directly because it ends an existing pregnancy before live birth. Around [73 million induced abortions](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/abortion) occur worldwide each year, according to the World Health Organization's estimate for 2015–2019. Guttmacher estimates [1,124,470 clinician-provided abortions](https://www.guttmacher.org/monthly-abortion-provision-study) in the United States in 2024.

Those numbers cannot simply be subtracted from births because some pregnancies would miscarry, while changes in law can affect sexual behavior, travel, and reporting. The underlying mechanism is still plain: historical evidence from American states that legalized abortion before *Roe v. Wade* found an estimated [5 percent decline in births](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5615) relative to comparison states, with later work finding that much of the reduction persisted in completed fertility.

American law changed sharply with *Roe* in 1973 and again when *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* [overruled *Roe*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf) in 2022. The moral argument is larger than demography. Abortion also ends the life of an unborn child, which is precisely why demographic language can become morally antiseptic here. A government that restricts abortion is not thereby forcing conception; it is making a judgment about whether an existing unborn life may be deliberately ended. Still, for the narrower causal question of this chapter, legal access to abortion lowers births.

## Trying to recover time

Assisted reproductive technology works in the other direction. In 2022, U.S. clinics reported [435,426 ART cycles and 94,039 live-birth deliveries](https://www.cdc.gov/art/php/national-summary/index.html). Every child conceived through treatment is fully equal in dignity and can be received with the same astonishment and love as any other child. The goodness of the child does not settle the morality of the method that produced him.

![Intracytoplasmic sperm injection of an oocyte](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/79/Icsi.JPG)

**A technique that cannot restore lost years** Intracytoplasmic sperm injection, one of the methods used in IVF laboratories. Treatment can help many infertile couples; success still falls sharply with the woman's age. *[via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Icsi.JPG)*
{: .caption}

What treatment cannot reliably restore is the reproductive time that passed before treatment began. In CDC's 2021 national table, intended egg-retrieval cycles using a patient's own eggs led to live birth in 50.8 percent of cases under age thirty-five, 36.4 percent at thirty-five to thirty-seven, 23.4 percent at thirty-eight to forty, and 7.9 percent over forty. Those are cycle-level figures rather than guarantees for a patient; the age pattern is hard to miss.

Medicine can preserve eggs and overcome some causes of infertility. It can also cross a line from healing the body to replacing the act by which a husband and wife conceive a child. Standard IVF commonly creates several embryos, subjects them to selection, freezes some for later use, and leaves others to be discarded or used in research. [*Donum Vitae* identifies the moral problem plainly](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19870222_respect-for-human-life_en.html): an embryo is already a human being with a claim to protection, not disposable biological material or one candidate among several.

The same concern applies to donor conception and surrogacy. They can satisfy an adult desire for a child by dividing parenthood among genetic, gestational, and social roles that the child did not choose. Infertility is a real suffering, and medicine should treat its causes wherever it can. A child remains a gift to receive rather than the object of a technical entitlement. Future technologies may push the biological boundary further. None makes biology morally optional.

Modern medicine gave people an astonishing ability to say “not yet.” It is much less capable of making “now” succeed after enough years have passed.
