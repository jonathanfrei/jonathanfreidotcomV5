Most of the causes in this chapter operate by changing choices, timing, or circumstances; biology is where those delays meet a harder limit. A couple can postpone trying for years and still feel as though the family remains available whenever they decide to begin. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the available time has already narrowed more than they realized.
{: .lede}

Female fertility is highest in the late teens and twenties, begins declining around thirty, and falls more rapidly in the later thirties. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine calls female age the [single most important predictor of fecundity](https://integration.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/fertility-evaluation-of-infertile-women-a-committee-opinion-2021/), with clinical guidance recommending infertility evaluation after twelve months of trying below age thirty-five, after six months at thirty-five or older, and more immediately above forty.

Those are population patterns, not a cliff at one birthday. Many women conceive easily after thirty-five, and some younger couples face infertility; the practical issue is family size. A couple hoping for one child can begin later than a couple hoping for three and still have a similar chance of reaching its goal. Delaying the first attempt from twenty-seven to thirty-seven changes more than the odds of that first birth. It removes much of the time available for another.

Men age reproductively too. Sperm production continues, but semen quality and sexual function can change, and advanced paternal age is associated with some pregnancy and offspring risks. The age effect is less abrupt than ovarian aging, but partnership still requires two people arriving at the same decision in time.

## When wanting is no longer enough

The World Health Organization estimates that [about one in six adults worldwide](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-04-2023-1-in-6-people-globally-affected-by-infertility/) experiences infertility at some point in life. In the United States, 13.4 percent of women ages fifteen to forty-nine had impaired fecundity in 2015–2019, while 8.5 percent of married women met the survey definition of infertility.

Some infertility would occur regardless of timing, but delay adds another source of loss. A couple can spend years carefully avoiding pregnancy, decide they are finally ready, and discover that wanting a child does not make conception happen on schedule. Reproductive medicine can help many of those couples, though its success also declines with age.

Health affects fertility in other ways. Obesity is associated with ovulatory dysfunction and lower natural fecundity and can impair male reproductive function. Smoking, some infections, and cancer treatment can also reduce the chance of conception. These causes matter for individual families without explaining the broad timing shift that has moved first births later across rich societies.

Research on a possible wider biological decline is more uncertain. A 2022 meta-analysis reported that sperm concentration among unselected men in its studies fell [51.6 percent between 1973 and 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36377604/). The finding deserves continued investigation, as do possible effects from endocrine-disrupting chemicals. Comparing semen samples across decades is difficult, however, and sperm count is not the same as births. I would not use a disputed biological trend to explain what delayed family formation already explains directly.

The central fact is simpler. Social timetables moved much faster than reproductive biology did: education lasts longer, marriage happens later, and careers take time to establish. Medicine has extended healthy life enormously, which can make thirty-five feel young in almost every other sense. Ovarian aging did not receive the same extension.

That mismatch helps explain one of the most painful forms of low fertility: the family that ends smaller than either parent intended. The next section tests the economic story from the opposite direction, by looking at societies where material scarcity is severe and births remain comparatively abundant.
