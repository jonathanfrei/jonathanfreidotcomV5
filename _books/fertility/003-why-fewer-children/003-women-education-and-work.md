title: Women, education, and work

Modern education and work opened choices to women that earlier generations often did not have. They also created a timing problem that is easy to describe and hard to solve: the years when a professional career asks for the most investment overlap heavily with the years when female fertility is highest.
{: .lede}

In the United States, women's labor-force participation rose from [32.7 percent in 1948](https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/womens-databook/2023/home.htm) to a peak of 60 percent in 1999 and stood at 57.3 percent in 2023. College and professional training expanded at the same time. A woman who finishes graduate school in her late twenties and then spends several years establishing herself at work may arrive at marriage and a first attempt to conceive much later than she once imagined.

The legal changes that opened employment were responses to real injustice. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 prohibited sex-based pay discrimination for substantially equal work. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited sex discrimination in employment. The Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 made clear that an employer could not penalize a woman because she was pregnant.

What law did not change was reproductive biology. Employers gradually built professional tracks around continuous availability through the twenties and thirties because that model had long fit male careers reasonably well. Women entered those tracks without receiving extra years in which to become educated, establish themselves, find a spouse, and have several children.

## The career clock

Paid work can make family formation easier. A secure job supplies income, independence, and confidence about the future. Across OECD countries, women's employment has been positively associated with fertility since the 1990s, especially where parents can combine work and family without one collapsing under the other.

The conflict becomes clearer after a birth. Henrik Kleven's [U.S. estimates](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2022number4/child-penalties-and-gender-inequality) show women's employment falling about 25 percent and earnings about 33 percent relative to men's after children arrive. Before parenthood, their earnings paths are much closer. The divergence begins with the child.

That average contains very different lives. Some mothers want to step back from paid work because caring for their children matters more to them. Others would prefer to remain home but cannot afford it. Others love their careers and still find that the household assumes they will absorb the sick day or missed meeting. The statistic cannot tell us which arrangement is good for a particular family. It does show that childbirth changes women's work much more than men's.

The timing effect comes before and after the birth. A woman may postpone children to protect the early years of a career, then reduce paid work once children arrive anyway. The delay can therefore preserve career continuity for a while without eliminating the eventual trade.

## Work at home

Mothers still do more childcare on average. Fathers do much more than fathers did in previous generations, but another child usually adds more work to the person already carrying more of it.

I do not think a good marriage can be reduced to equal minutes on a spreadsheet. Couples have different jobs, temperaments, and seasons. The relevant question is whether the arrangement leaves both adults able to keep the promises they have made without one person's work, paid or unpaid, becoming invisible.

The larger cultural problem is that care is often treated as time taken away from the serious work of adulthood. A résumé records the promotion but not the years spent raising three competent children. That status difference affects choices long before anyone calculates a fertility rate.

There is no reason to give up women's education or meaningful work. The harder task is to stop organizing both as though family formation can be postponed indefinitely without cost. A society that asks women to fit motherhood around a career designed for a different biological clock should not be surprised when many families begin late and end small.
