---
title: The economics of children
deck: Prosperity can make children easier to support while raising the threshold for feeling ready to have them
eyebrow: Money and time
---

The economic problem is not simply that children cost money. Rich societies have more resources than any generation before them. The harder problem is that adulthood now comes with expensive thresholds before a child feels prudent: an independent home, stable work, enough income to absorb childcare, and enough time to parent according to modern expectations.
{: .lede}

Housing often comes first. Couples wait to move out of a shared apartment, leave their parents' home, or buy in a neighborhood where they would feel comfortable raising children. The [OECD's 2024 review](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/society-at-a-glance-2024_918d8db3-en/full-report/fertility-trends-across-the-oecd-underlying-drivers-and-the-role-for-policy_770679b8.html) found rising housing expenditure associated with lower fertility across member countries. Southern Europe combines expensive housing, late household formation, and very low fertility. Housing does not explain all of that, but the practical sequence is easy to recognize: no independent home, no marriage, no baby.

Childcare adds another threshold after the first birth. Parents need it when both incomes matter, and those incomes matter partly because housing and formal care are expensive. A scarce slot or unreliable schedule can turn a second child from something desired into something that feels financially reckless. Countries with a smoother bridge from parental leave into affordable childcare generally make work and family easier to combine. Even there, fertility has recently fallen, so childcare removes a barrier without solving the whole problem.

## The cost off the books

The larger economic cost is often time. An hour spent caring for a child is an hour not spent earning, resting, or advancing at work. That trade becomes more expensive as education and wages rise.

Mothers still absorb more of the direct care. In the [2024 American Time Use Survey](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/atus_06262025.htm), women living with children under eighteen averaged 1.79 hours a day of primary childcare, compared with 1.11 for men. With a child under six, women spent about three hours and men two. Those averages still miss much of the background labor: the sick-day pickup, the appointment remembered, the schedule rearranged.

A working parent may decline travel or move into a more flexible role. The cost then appears years later in slower wage growth or retirement savings. It falls most heavily on the parent whose career bends, still usually the mother.

Higher earnings therefore pull in both directions. They make diapers and rent easier to pay. They also make time away from work more expensive. The path to becoming affluent can consume the same years in which a couple hoped to have several children.

## When enough keeps moving

Parents in wealthy societies also spend more on each child because the standard of a good childhood has risen. A family may feel responsible for the better school district, the organized activity, and eventually college. None of those choices is absurd. Together they can turn four affordable children into two expensive ones.

Economic insecurity creates delay even when lifetime income is adequate. A couple waits for the permanent job, the lower mortgage rate, or the year that finally feels stable. Waiting can be rational one year at a time and still leave less room for the family they wanted.

The [OECD evidence](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/society-at-a-glance-2024_918d8db3-en/full-report/fertility-trends-across-the-oecd-underlying-drivers-and-the-role-for-policy_770679b8.html) reflects that tension. Employment can support fertility by providing income and confidence, while housing expense and insecurity push it down. Money matters, but “affordable” is not a fixed number. It depends on what a couple believes must be in place before a child can arrive.

Rich countries should be able to make that threshold lower than they do. The next question is why the years required to reach it so often overlap with the years in which women are building the education and careers modern adulthood expects.
