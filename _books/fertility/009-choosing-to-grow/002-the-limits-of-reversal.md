---
title: The Limits of Reversal
deck: Government can clear a path to family life, though it cannot walk that path for anyone
eyebrow: Conclusion
---

Government should make it easier for people to form the families they already have reason to want. That is a substantial job. It is also a bounded one.
{: .lede}

A home with room for a child has to be within reach. Work should leave space for pregnancy, infancy, and the disorderly years that follow. Paid leave and reliable childcare can keep a wanted child from becoming a choice between family and economic security. These policies work best when they are durable enough for a couple to build plans around them rather than generous enough to make a good announcement.

The state cannot supply the rest of family life. It cannot introduce a future spouse, make a marriage trustworthy, move grandparents closer, or persuade an exhausted mother that the work she is doing is honorable. It cannot give back fertile years that passed while two people waited to feel completely ready. By the time a family applies for a childcare benefit, many of the decisions that determine its eventual size have already been made.

That is why the choice in this chapter is distributed among many people. A city decides whether a young couple can afford to live near work and family. An employer decides whether motherhood quietly ends a career. Parents decide what picture of adulthood they give their children. Churches and other voluntary communities can become places where unmarried people actually know one another and where new parents have someone to call on Tuesday afternoon. Families decide whether proximity is worth more than the highest possible salary somewhere else.

Government still has a boundary it must not cross. China demonstrated the evil of treating births as production quotas when the one-child policy intruded into marriage, pregnancy, and the body. Concern about low fertility does not justify the same mistake in reverse. Children should be welcomed as children, not produced for a target, and adults remain responsible for choices no ministry can rightfully make for them.

UNFPA's 2025 survey across fourteen countries found that one in five respondents expected to miss their desired number of children. The same research found people pressured toward children they did not want. Both experiences deserve to be taken seriously. A free society can remove barriers to wanted family life without turning fertility into an order.

I think that is a better standard than asking whether government can engineer a particular birth rate. We should ask whether more people can meet, commit, begin earlier if they want to, and have the children they hoped for without accepting needless poverty or exile from public life. Policy can clear some of that path. Most of the walking happens elsewhere.
