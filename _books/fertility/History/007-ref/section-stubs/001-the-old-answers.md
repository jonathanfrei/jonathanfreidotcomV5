Societies worried about continuity have not always waited for consent. Their stories and policies often move directly from the need for descendants to control over marriage, sex, and women's bodies. The record belongs in this book because it exposes the temptation inside demographic urgency, not because it supplies a usable playbook.

## Drafting notes (not yet prose)

The introduction's cold open was a glimpse. This section stays with historical extremes long enough to learn from them. Same rubric as a baby bonus: mechanism, who was allowed to reproduce, who paid, what was destroyed along with whatever births arrived. Deadpan, not a guilt exhibit.

### The kidnapping of the Sabine women

- Separate Livy's foundation legend from recoverable eighth-century history. The episode is myth used as origin story.
- Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita* 1.9–13: refusal of *conubium*; Consualia; seizure of maidens; Romulus's speech; later interpolation of the women between armies; fusion of peoples.
- Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*, as a second ancient telling.
- What the story assumes about women, marriage, conquest, assimilation, and civic continuity.
- Why demographic necessity is presented as capable of transforming violence into founding legend.
- Vocabulary: English "Rape of the Sabine Women" translates *raptus* (seizure/abduction).

### Germany before and during the Second World War

- Separate Weimar-era population anxiety from Nazi family, marriage, abortion, sterilization, and racial policy.
- Marriage loans (*Ehestandsdarlehen*), Law for the Reduction of Unemployment, 1 June 1933: interest-free loan up to 1,000 RM in household vouchers; originally the bride had to leave paid work; one quarter forgiven per live child (*abkindern*); eligibility restricted by "Aryan" descent, hereditary health, and political reliability.
- Honour Cross of the German Mother (1938/1939): bronze / silver / gold for four, six, and eight children; no cash; Hitler Youth ordered to salute wearers.
- Kindergeld and other family money for approved families.
- Restrictions on women's work as labor-market and fertility policy at once.
- Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring (14 July 1933): compulsory sterilization of those labeled hereditarily ill; abortion policy split between the "valuable" and those marked for elimination.
- Lebensborn and the division between births the regime encouraged and lives it marked for destruction.
- A policy can raise or redirect some births while corrupting the meaning of family and human equality.
- Do not treat pronatalism as separable from the regime's racial hierarchy and coercive state.
- Empirical caution: births rose in the mid-1930s from Depression lows; completed fertility did not become a high-fertility society; war then collapsed the rate. Magnitude is disputed; the selection of *who* may reproduce is not.

### Analytical purpose

Each case against the same questions: Who was allowed to choose? Who bore the cost? Which lives counted? What did the state or story call a family? Did the apparent demographic result depend on violence, exclusion, or conditions that cannot be abstracted from it?
