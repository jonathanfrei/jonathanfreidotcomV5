Many extreme proposals begin from a real constraint and answer it badly. Housing is scarce, care consumes time, careers punish interruption, and raising children competes with the income required to support them. Abundance may not remove the moral question, but it can change which tradeoffs remain necessary. Managed decline is not costless either.

## Drafting notes (not yet prose)

Merge of the former "Abundance changes the bargain" and "What is worse than decline?" files. Close toward the next chapter's two paths. No program.

### Forms of abundance to explore

- Cheap energy, automated construction, and a much larger supply of family-sized housing.
- AI and automation that reduce necessary paid labor without making human care disposable.
- Healthcare that heals infertility and protects pregnancy without industrializing reproduction.
- Education that becomes less expensive, less credential-bound, and easier to combine with early family life.
- Transport and remote work that allow extended families to remain geographically close.
- Public wealth or social dividends that recognize caregiving without making parents employees of the state.

### Recovering mechanisms from bad proposals

These cousins do not require the reader to have accepted the bad vehicle:

- Material room for a one-earner household is the non-coercive cousin of "stop hiring women."
- Visible honor is the cousin of "laugh at the girlboss."
- Pension credit for childrearing is the cousin of a childless tax.
- Institutions that help people meet and commit are the cousin of state matchmaking.
- Medicine ordered toward healing is the cousin of industrial reproduction.
- Plural experiments people can enter, leave, imitate, or reject are the cousin of a uniform central plan.

### Questions about abundance

- Does abundance raise fertility, or merely make low-fertility individualism more comfortable?
- Which constraints are genuinely material and which are cultural or relational?
- Can benefits be durable enough for families to trust without becoming politically impossible?
- What forms of abundance strengthen marriage and kinship rather than substituting services for them?

### The comparison with decline

- Distinguish temporary hardship from permanent generational contraction.
- Identify losses that adaptation can absorb and losses that become irreversible.
- Compare concentrated harms imposed by a remedy with diffuse harms imposed by decline.
- Ask who bears each cost, who consents, who can exit, and who is never represented in the decision (including future people).
- Separate politically unpopular sacrifice from violations of human dignity.
- Failure to act can be an injustice toward children, the poor, the elderly, or successors. It still does not license domination.

### Questions at the edge

- How much wealth, leisure, autonomy, or inherited entitlement should a society surrender to make family life possible?
- When does taxation for family support become unjust, and when does refusing support simply hide costs inside households?
- Could severe limits on consumption or geographic mobility be preferable to demographic contraction?
- Which institutions are worth preserving, and which should be allowed to disappear rather than impose an immoral remedy?
- Can a civilization be lost through demographic passivity?
- Can it also be lost by surviving through means that corrupt its understanding of the person?

### Intended landing

The chapter should not end with an extreme program. Decline has real victims and may demand sacrifices larger than contemporary politics admits. Some measures can be reconsidered under abundance or voluntary association. Other measures remain wrong even if they work. The task is to become capable of more than managed decline without making continuity an excuse for domination. Then hand the reader to the two paths.
