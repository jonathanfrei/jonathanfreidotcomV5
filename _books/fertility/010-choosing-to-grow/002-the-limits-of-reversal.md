title: What policy can do
deck: Government can clear a path to family life without pretending it can walk that path for anyone
eyebrow: Public responsibility

No wealthy country has found a reliable government formula for restoring very low fertility to replacement and keeping it there. That verdict limits what policy can promise. It does not make government irrelevant.
{: .lede}

Government can remove barriers, protect workers from being punished for pregnancy or care, share costs families now carry privately, and keep declining places within reach of essential services. Support should be durable enough to enter real plans and early enough to matter before a household already exists. These actions can improve family life and sometimes recover births without restoring replacement fertility.

The [policy record developed earlier](/books/fertility/reverse-fertility-decline/introduction) gives those efforts a more exact standard. A program may improve family life, help parents fulfill an intention, shift timing, or change completed family size. Those are different results, and a government should claim only the one it can demonstrate.

Timing matters as much as generosity. A payment after birth cannot repair years spent unable to find a suitable home or combine training with family life. Governments reach upstream through housing rules, education, transportation, labor standards, and public services, while recognizing that none can supply the relationships and commitments from which families begin.

Public authority's bounded work is to clear barriers, allocate inherited burdens, protect rights, and sustain common goods. It cannot supply the intimate relationships from which family life grows, and it cannot turn an annual rate into durable renewal.

Public action also needs a stable horizon. [OECD reviews of family policy](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/doing-better-for-families_9789264098732-en.html) have emphasized coherence across programs and continuity over a child's life, because adults make family plans against support they expect to remain. Stability does not require preserving every program. It requires dependable commitments and evaluation over the years in which births, care, and completed family size unfold.

Policy succeeds first when it removes a needless burden, protects parents and children, and manages obligations inherited from the older age structure. Durable cohort recovery is a further result, not the only one that makes support worthwhile. Government can clear part of the path; the positive work of renewal depends on institutions and relationships closer to the household.
