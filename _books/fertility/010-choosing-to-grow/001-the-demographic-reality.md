A baby changes a household immediately and a population slowly. For years, she needs more care than she can return. Only later does she enter the adult world of work, taxes, invention, parenthood, and care for other people. Demographic recovery begins with present obligations and becomes public capacity decades afterward.

That delay governs the conclusion of this book. In 2024, more than half of the world's countries and areas had fertility below the level needed for long-run replacement without migration. Nearly one fifth were below 1.4 births per woman. The people who will be old in the 2040s are already alive, as are most of the adults who will provide their care and finance public promises during those years. A fertility recovery can change the generation being born. It cannot insert missing adults into the age structure.

The first duty is therefore adaptation. Older people do not lose their claims because too few children were born after them, and smaller towns do not cease to deserve competent government. Pensions will need financing, care will need hands, and infrastructure built for a larger population will have to be maintained, consolidated, or repurposed. The [OECD's work on shrinking regions](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/shrinking-smartly-and-sustainably_f91693e3-en.html) treats adaptation and efforts to mitigate decline as complementary responses shaped by local conditions. That is the right frame. People living through demographic change should not be made to wait for a recovery that cannot help them soon.

Adaptation has a second duty: it should not consume the possibility of renewal. A smaller working-age generation can be asked to finance every inherited promise, provide more unpaid care, and maintain systems built for more users. If those demands leave young adults without time, housing, or confidence to begin families, a humane response to one generation can deepen the problem for the next. The settlement between old and young has to protect both.

This is why the argument runs on two clocks. Over the next few decades, societies must manage an age structure they cannot repeal. Over a generation and beyond, decisions about marriage, work, housing, care, and children can change which cohorts follow. The first clock calls for realism and solidarity. The second leaves room for choice.

We cannot rebuild the population of 2040 from scratch. We can decide whether the years after it inherit another round of contraction or the difficult beginning of renewal.
