A baby changes a household immediately and a population slowly. The conclusion begins with that mismatch: care is owed now, while any demographic recovery becomes public capacity decades later.
{: .lede}

![World fertility rate, 1950–2023](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/children-per-woman.png?tab=chart&country=~OWID_WRL)

**The inherited curve** Births per woman, world average, UN estimates through 2023. In 2024, more than half of countries and areas were below replacement fertility even while the global average remained near two. *[Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/children-per-woman)*
{: .caption}

In 2024, more than half of the world's countries and areas were below replacement fertility, and nearly one fifth were below 1.4 births per woman. The people who will be old in the 2040s are already alive, as are most of the adults who will provide their care. A fertility recovery can change the generation being born. It cannot insert missing adults into the age structure.

The two clocks established earlier now become one obligation. Societies must adapt humanely to the age structure they cannot repeal while refusing to load every inherited cost onto the adults who might form the next generation. Older people retain their claims; younger households need time, housing, and confidence; shrinking places still require competent government. Adaptation and renewal meet in the settlement between them.

The demographic inheritance is fixed enough to require solidarity and open enough to require choice. The next question is what public authority can contribute without pretending to control the result.
