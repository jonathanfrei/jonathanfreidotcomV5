The people who will be thirty years old in 2056 are beginning to be born now, and in many countries there will be far fewer of them than the thirty-year-olds alive today. Nothing can fill in the cohorts already missing. The question is what today's adaptation will make possible after them.

Two paths can begin from the same inherited age structure. Renewal uses adaptation to preserve capacity while family formation becomes more attainable, births recover, and generations move toward balance. Contraction uses adaptation to make a smaller society work. It first **consolidates**—schools combine, services centralize, machines cover scarce labor—and then **selects**, as each smaller generation decides which places, institutions, relationships, and ambitions still receive successors.

Both paths owe humane care to the people already here. They differ in what adaptation protects. Four kinds of evidence reveal the direction before population totals do: demographic recovery, household formation and wellbeing, institutional succession, and the **margin** left after care and maintenance for new families and uncertain work. A temporary rise in births is not renewal if later cohorts remain smaller. A balanced budget is not successful adaptation if young households and local institutions lose the capacity to begin.

No country chooses either path in a single vote. The direction emerges from millions of family decisions and from the homes, workplaces, schools, budgets, laws, and expectations surrounding them. Regions can diverge, recovery can stall, and societies can turn. These are paths rather than forecasts. The pages ahead test both directions against the same dashboard: first renewal, then capable contraction, then contraction that begins selecting its own future, and finally the thresholds at which maintenance consumes the margin for beginnings.

Renewal comes first. Births recover, and almost nothing gets easier right away.
