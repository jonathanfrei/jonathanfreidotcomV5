For most of my life, population growth felt like part of the background of the world. Cities spread, schools filled, new workers arrived, and every argument about the future began with the assumption that there would be more people living in it. That assumption is starting to weaken. The [United Nations estimated](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/world-population-prospects-2024-summary-results-0) the global fertility rate at 2.25 births per woman in 2024, down from 3.31 in 1990, with fertility below the long-run replacement level in more than half of all countries and areas. One quarter of humanity already lives in a country whose population has peaked.
{: .lede}

The world as a whole is still growing. The same UN projection puts the global peak around 10.3 billion people in the mid-2080s, followed by a gradual decline to about 10.2 billion in 2100. Those numbers are large enough to make the language of population decline sound premature. But global totals can hide a change that is already well underway: in many countries, fewer children are entering schools, fewer young adults are entering work and family life, and a growing share of the population is moving into old age.

I don't think this is simply a story about fewer births. It is a change in the way families are formed, work is organized, care is given, institutions are financed, and societies imagine their future. A birth today becomes a student, a worker, a parent, a neighbor, a caregiver, and perhaps the person who carries some inherited craft or institution into another generation. When births fall for many years, the effects arrive slowly and then appear almost everywhere at once.

## The arithmetic becomes human

Population ageing is partly the result of something very good: people live longer. It is also shaped by sustained low fertility. The [UN Population Division](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/ageing-1) describes the sequence plainly. As fertility first falls, the working-age share of a population can rise and create a demographic dividend. Later, the age distribution shifts upward. By 2050, [the UN expects](https://www.un.org/en/global-issues/ageing) roughly one person in six worldwide to be over 65.

Ratios do not care for anyone, though people do. A rising old-age dependency ratio eventually means more retirees relative to conventional working ages, but it does not tell us how healthy those retirees will be, how long people will work, how productive each worker will become, or how families and communities will share care. The [UN's own work on ageing](https://desapublications.un.org/publications/world-social-report-2023-leaving-no-one-behind-ageing-world) cautions that traditional dependency measures can overstate the burden when longer lives change what a fixed age threshold means. Demography sets conditions. It does not dictate the quality of our response.

Even so, conditions matter. Pension systems need contributors. Hospitals and nursing homes need staff. Children need parents, teachers, and neighborhoods with enough life in them to endure. Scientific fields need new researchers, and institutions need successors who can understand what they have inherited before deciding what to change. Automation can help with some of this. Immigration can change the scale and age structure of a country. Longer working lives, better health, and higher productivity can do a great deal too. None of them makes the underlying generational question disappear.

That question is whether a society can continue to flourish when each generation is smaller than the one before it. I mean flourishing in a fairly ordinary sense: people able to form the families they want, care for those who depend on them, do useful work, preserve knowledge, build worthwhile institutions, and leave their children more freedom and possibility than they inherited.

## A real benefit, and a real trade-off

A slower-growing population can ease pressure on land, water, habitat, energy, and the climate. The UN has said that an earlier and lower population peak could reduce environmental pressure through lower aggregate consumption. The [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-2/) likewise identifies both rising income and population growth as drivers of emissions, while finding that growth in consumption associated with rising incomes has had the larger effect in recent decades.

It would be strange to write about fertility decline without taking that benefit seriously. A smaller population may make some environmental goals easier. It may also allow crowded places to become less expensive or give damaged ecosystems room to recover. Population is not the only driver of environmental harm, however, and often it is not the strongest one. A smaller affluent population can consume more than a larger poor one. Technology, energy systems, institutions, and habits of consumption determine whether fewer people actually produce a lighter footprint.

The trade-off is not between a crowded planet and an abstract desire for more births. It is between goods that can pull in different directions. Lower aggregate consumption may relieve pressure on nature. Fewer young people may also reduce the human capacity available to repair ecological damage, maintain complex infrastructure, discover cleaner technologies, and respond to risks we cannot yet see. I don't assume that more people automatically solve those problems. I do think human beings are the source of every solution we have found so far.

## Freedom and continuity

Any serious discussion of fertility has to begin with the dignity and freedom of the people whose lives are being counted. A government may want more births, an economist may want more workers, and a pension system may need more contributors. None of those needs gives the state ownership of a person's body or family. People are not inputs into a demographic plan.

They are also not isolated choices floating outside every institution. The [United Nations Population Fund's 2025 survey](https://www.unfpa.org/swp2025), conducted in 14 countries containing more than a third of the world's population, found that nearly one in five reproductive-age adults expected not to have the number of children they desired. Financial limits, housing, insecure work, difficulty finding a suitable partner, health, and fear about the future all appeared as barriers. The survey cannot speak for every person in every country, but it complicates the easy claim that low fertility is simply modern freedom expressing itself. Sometimes people are choosing smaller families. Sometimes the life they hoped to build has become difficult to reach.

I want to hold both truths together. Reproductive freedom deserves protection, including the freedom not to have children. A society also has reason to care when many people cannot form the families they want, and when the accumulated result is a shrinking base of children, workers, caregivers, citizens, and future parents.

The question becomes larger as fertility decline spreads. A country can draw workers from younger places. A region can buy technology and knowledge created elsewhere. A generation can spend inherited capital while adapting its institutions. Those options become narrower if low fertility turns into a durable global condition. Humanity would then have to sustain scientific progress, cultural memory, institutional competence, planetary defense, and any eventual expansion beyond Earth with a population that is not merely older or smaller, but repeatedly contracting.

I don't know where that threshold lies, or whether there is one number that could define it. This book will not pretend to know. Its central question is more practical: can we make family formation compatible with modern freedom and prosperity while learning to live well through the demographic transition already underway? The answer will shape more than the size of the economy. It will shape how much of the future we remain able to build.
