---
title: The Civilizational Stakes of a Shrinking Humanity
eyebrow: The fertility decline
---

The human population is still growing, but the direction beneath that growth has changed. The [United Nations now projects](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/world-population-prospects-2024-summary-results-0) a global peak within this century, and one quarter of the world's people already live in countries whose populations have peaked. In much of the world, the familiar fear that there will always be more people is giving way to a quieter question: what happens when each generation is smaller than the last?
{: .lede}

I began with that question because fertility decline reaches into nearly every part of life while remaining easy to see as somebody else's problem. A falling rate becomes an empty desk in a school, a delayed marriage, a missing worker, an adult child caring for two parents, or a town deciding which public services it can still keep. Decades later, those private and local changes alter the age, capacity, and ambitions of a country.

There are benefits as well as costs. Slower population growth can reduce pressure on land, resources, and the climate. Longer lives are an achievement, not a burden to regret. Smaller societies may remain prosperous, humane, and inventive. I do not want to assume that growth is always good or that decline is always bad.

I do want to ask what sustained decline requires of us. Can we help people form the families they want without turning parenthood into a public duty? Can productivity, immigration, technology, and healthier old age allow societies to adapt? If fertility remains low almost everywhere, can humanity continue to preserve knowledge, care for the vulnerable, build durable institutions, and take on projects whose rewards lie beyond one lifetime?

The first step is to state that question clearly. Then we can decide what evidence might actually answer it.
