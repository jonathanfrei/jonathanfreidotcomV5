Fertility decline does not have one cause, and I don't expect this book to find one. People have fewer children for different reasons in different places. Some changes are deliberate. Others emerge from ordinary decisions that only look demographic when millions of people make them at once.
{: .lede}

The book follows that accumulation from the household outward. It asks how family formation became later and harder in many rich societies, how modern reproductive control changed the number of births, and what happens when those choices compound across generations.

No single mechanism explains every country. The point is to understand how the main ones combine, where the evidence is strong, and which constraints people might actually be able to change.

## Why fertility fell

The first part of the book looks backward. The demographic transition began with one of the great achievements of modern life: far fewer children died. Population surged because death rates fell before birth rates did. For much of the twentieth century, the result looked like a permanent population explosion. The next chapter traces how quickly that expectation changed.

From there, the book moves closer to ordinary life. Contraception prevents conception. Abortion ends pregnancies before live birth. Later marriage reduces the number of reproductive years spent in a stable partnership. Paid work can compete with childbearing for time, especially when careers are built around the same years in which female fertility is highest. Other conditions matter too, but they need not all be listed here to make the point: low fertility is produced by several mechanisms acting together.

The aim is not to turn every correlation into a cause. Paid work, for example, can both affect fertility and be affected by it. Women who expect larger families may choose different work patterns before the first child arrives. Later chapters will separate these relationships where the evidence allows it.

I also don't want complexity to become an excuse for avoiding plain mechanisms. Modern reproductive control has changed fertility because it allows people to avoid births that would once have occurred. The [United Nations estimates](https://desapublications.un.org/policy-briefs/un-desa-policy-brief-no-172-new-landscape-fertility-and-family-planning-30-years) that the number of women using modern contraceptive methods rose from 552 million in 1994 to 871 million in 2024. The [World Health Organization estimates](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/abortion) roughly 73 million induced abortions worldwide each year. These facts do not settle the moral or legal arguments around either practice. They belong in an account of why fewer children are born.

## What low fertility changes

The next question is what happens after low fertility persists long enough to change the age structure. Some consequences are easy to count, such as fewer students and a larger share of older people. Others are visible first in ordinary institutions: a family has fewer relatives to share care, or a town loses the next owner of a local business.

Rich societies are adaptable. Scarce labor can encourage automation, and longer healthy lives can make old categories of worker and retiree less useful. A smaller population may also ease some environmental pressures. Demographic decline creates incentives to solve familiar problems differently.

The question is how far adaptation can carry us when the underlying direction continues. A country can close a school because there are fewer children and still educate them well. It can automate a factory and produce more with fewer workers. If each generation is smaller than the one before it, however, the adjustment never quite ends. The society becomes very good at managing contraction while the base for the next round narrows again.

## Whether fertility can rise again

Governments have spent decades trying to influence fertility, sometimes coercively and sometimes through benefits meant to make family life easier. The record is uncomfortable. States have often been effective at reducing births. They have been much less successful at restoring replacement fertility once very low fertility becomes established.

That does not make family policy pointless. A childcare subsidy can help a family even if the national fertility rate barely moves. Housing reform can make adulthood easier without producing an immediate baby boom. Later chapters will judge policies first by what they actually do, not by whether they produce an impressive headline number.

The harder work may lie beyond policy. People usually do not decide whether to have children after consulting a national fertility target. They decide inside a life: whether they have a partner, enough stability, and confidence that another child will fit inside the household they are building. Law can change some of those conditions. Culture and family networks shape others.

## Adaptation is not the same as renewal

The final part of the book separates two tasks that are often blended together. The first is adaptation. Much of the aging of the next few decades is already fixed because the people who will be old then are alive now and the adults who will support them have mostly been born. Governments and families will have to manage that reality whether fertility rises tomorrow or not.

The [OECD's work on aging populations](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/10/ageing-populations-their-fiscal-implications-and-policy-responses_be4bd619/6aec03b3-en.pdf) points toward longer working lives and productivity growth as ways to make the transition less painful. Immigration can add workers quickly. These measures can buy time.

The second task is renewal. Immigration can help one country while moving people out of another. Machines can increase output without becoming descendants. A later retirement age can improve a pension system without creating the adults who will be alive a century from now. Adaptation can extend capacity. It does not answer whether the next generation will be large enough to replace the one before it.

I don't think the goal should be to maximize births or to pick an ideal population size. The standard I use throughout the book is closer to continuity: can free people form the families they hope for, can older societies adapt without becoming cruel or stagnant, and can civilization retain enough human depth to pass on what it has inherited?

There is plenty of room for disagreement about how much fertility can be changed and how much adaptation can accomplish. I have views on those questions, but I would rather earn them through the evidence than announce them here.

The starting point is narrower. More than half of the world's countries and areas are already below replacement fertility. The old assumption that each generation would naturally be larger than the last can no longer be taken for granted. The rest of the book is an attempt to understand what replaced it.
