Fertility decline does not have one cause, and I don't expect this book to find one. People have fewer children for different reasons in different places. Some changes are deliberate, some are constrained, and some emerge from several ordinary decisions that only look demographic when millions of people make them at once.
{: .lede}

The book follows that accumulation from the household outward. It asks why people marry later or not at all, why the cost of a home can change the timing of a first child, why contraception and abortion alter the number of births, why paid work competes with childbearing for years that biology does not replace, and why cultural expectations can make parenthood feel either like a normal part of adult life or an unusually difficult project.

None of those mechanisms explains every country. They do not need to. The point is to understand how they combine, where the evidence is strong, where it is mixed, and which constraints people might actually be able to change.

## Why fertility fell

The first part of the book looks backward. The demographic transition began with one of the great achievements of modern life: far fewer children died. Population surged because death rates fell before birth rates did. For much of the twentieth century, the result looked like a permanent population explosion. The next chapter traces how quickly that expectation changed.

From there, the book moves closer to ordinary life. Contraception prevents conception. Abortion ends pregnancies before live birth. Later marriage leaves fewer reproductive years within a stable partnership. Education and paid employment can open valuable opportunities while also competing with childbearing for time, especially when careers are built around the same years in which female fertility is highest. Housing, childcare, dating, health, religion, family networks, and expectations about what a good parent owes a child all shape the decision further.

The aim is not to turn every correlation into a cause. Paid work, for example, can both affect fertility and be affected by it. Women who expect larger families may choose different work patterns before the first child arrives. A country with expensive housing may also have late marriage, dense cities, long commutes, and demanding labor markets. Later chapters will separate these relationships where the evidence allows it.

I also don't want complexity to become an excuse for avoiding plain mechanisms. Modern reproductive control has changed fertility because it allows people to avoid births that would once have occurred. The [United Nations estimates](https://desapublications.un.org/policy-briefs/un-desa-policy-brief-no-172-new-landscape-fertility-and-family-planning-30-years) that the number of women using modern contraceptive methods rose from 552 million in 1994 to 871 million in 2024. The [World Health Organization estimates](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/abortion) roughly 73 million induced abortions worldwide each year. These facts do not settle the moral, legal, or policy arguments around either practice. They belong in an account of why fewer children are born.

## What low fertility changes

The next question is what happens after low fertility persists long enough to change the age structure. Some consequences are relatively easy to count: fewer students, a smaller working-age population, a higher share of older people, more pressure on pensions and care, and shifts in housing demand. Others are harder to measure but just as real. Families have fewer siblings, aunts, uncles, and cousins. Towns lose the people who would have inherited businesses or volunteered at the fire department. Organizations can find themselves with money and technology but too few successors who understand the work.

I am wary of turning every inconvenience into evidence of civilizational collapse. Rich societies are adaptable. Scarce labor can raise wages and encourage automation. Empty buildings can be repurposed. Longer healthy lives can make the old categories of worker and retiree less useful. A smaller population may ease some environmental pressures. Demographic decline creates losses, but it also creates incentives to solve problems differently.

The question is how far adaptation can carry us when the underlying direction continues. A country can close a school because there are fewer children and still educate them well. It can combine two hospitals and still provide excellent care. It can automate a factory and produce more with fewer workers. If each generation is smaller than the one before it, however, the adjustment never quite ends. The society becomes very good at managing contraction while the base for the next round of management narrows again.

## Whether fertility can rise again

Governments have spent decades trying to influence fertility, sometimes coercively and sometimes through benefits meant to make family life easier. The record is uncomfortable. States have often been effective at reducing births. They have been much less successful at restoring replacement fertility once very low fertility becomes established.

That does not make family policy pointless. A childcare subsidy can help a family even if the national fertility rate barely moves. Housing reform can make adulthood easier without producing an immediate baby boom. Paid leave can keep parents attached to work. Tax policy can reduce some of the penalty attached to raising children. Later chapters will judge these policies first by what they actually do, not by whether they produce an impressive headline number.

The harder work may lie beyond policy. People usually do not decide whether to have children after consulting a national fertility target. They decide inside a life: whether they have a partner, enough space, a stable income, relatives nearby, confidence that another child will not wreck the household, and a sense that parenthood belongs inside the kind of adulthood they hope to build. Law and policy can change some of those conditions. Employers, families, neighborhoods, churches, schools, housing markets, and culture shape others.

## Adaptation is not the same as renewal

The final part of the book separates two tasks that are often blended together. The first is adaptation. Much of the aging of the next few decades is already fixed because the people who will be old then are alive now and the adults who will support them have mostly been born. Governments and families will have to manage that reality whether fertility rises tomorrow or not.

The [OECD's work on aging populations](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/10/ageing-populations-their-fiscal-implications-and-policy-responses_be4bd619/6aec03b3-en.pdf) points toward longer working lives, pension reform, productivity growth, and other adjustments that can make the transition less painful. Immigration can add workers quickly. Automation can multiply what each worker can do. Better health can reduce the years in which older people need intensive support.

The second task is renewal. Immigration can help one country while moving people out of another. Machines can increase output without becoming descendants. A later retirement age can improve a pension system without creating the adults who will be alive a century from now. These tools buy time and capacity. They do not answer whether the next generation will be large enough to replace the one before it.

I don't think the goal should be to maximize births or to pick an ideal population size. The standard I use throughout the book is closer to continuity: can free people form the families they hope for, can older societies adapt without becoming cruel or stagnant, and can civilization keep enough human depth to pass on what it has inherited and attempt work that lasts longer than one lifetime?

That leaves plenty of room for disagreement. How much fertility can policy change? How much should it try? How far can technology compensate for fewer workers? How much population decline could a rich society absorb while remaining prosperous? I have views on those questions, but I would rather earn them through the evidence than announce them here.

The starting point is narrower. More than half of the world's countries and areas are already below replacement fertility. The old assumption that each generation would naturally be larger than the last can no longer be taken for granted. The rest of the book is an attempt to understand what replaced it.
