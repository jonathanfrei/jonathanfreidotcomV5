A classroom does not disappear all at once. One year there are thirty children in the first grade. A few years later there are twenty-four. Then twenty. Eventually the district combines grades, closes a building, and sends the remaining students farther down the road. Nothing dramatic happened on the day the first smaller class arrived. The change only became obvious after the missing children had worked their way through the school.
{: .lede}

Demographic decline works like that. It begins with births, then moves through everything built around the expectation that another generation will arrive: schools, neighborhoods, workplaces, hospitals, pension systems, churches, armies, laboratories, families. The effects come slowly enough that each year's change can look manageable. By the time the pattern is unmistakable, much of it has already been set in motion.

For most of the modern period, the demographic fear ran in the other direction. The question was whether there would be too many people for the planet to support. That fear shaped politics, environmentalism, development policy, and popular imagination for generations. The human population is still growing today, but the direction underneath that growth has changed. The [United Nations now projects](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/world-population-prospects-2024-summary-results-0) a global peak around 10.3 billion people in the mid-2080s, followed by gradual decline. More than half of countries and areas are already below the long-run replacement level, and one quarter of humanity lives in places whose populations have peaked.

The global total can make this sound less urgent than it is. Population has momentum. The people having children today were born twenty or thirty years ago, when cohorts were larger in many places. Longer lives also keep the total population growing after births have fallen. A country can therefore keep getting bigger for years while the generation underneath it is already shrinking.

The arithmetic becomes clearer if we ignore forecasts for a moment and use a simple illustration. In a low-mortality population, replacement fertility is roughly 2.1 births per woman. If fertility settles at 1.5 and stays there, each generation is roughly 71 percent the size of the one before it, before accounting for migration. Start with a generation of 100 people. The next is about 71. Then 51. Then 36. Then 26. By the fifth generation, the cohort is around 19 people.

That is not a forecast for any particular country. Fertility changes, people migrate, survival improves, and real populations are more complicated. It is simply what repeated below-replacement fertility does if nothing interrupts it. A number that sounds modest in one year becomes a very different society when it compounds across generations.

This is why I don't think falling fertility is mainly an argument about whether a country should have 50 million people or 70 million. There is no ideal population number that can be pulled from a chart. Smaller societies can be prosperous, peaceful, inventive, and beautiful. Slower population growth can reduce pressure on land, resources, and the climate. Longer lives are an achievement. None of that changes the direction of the arithmetic. A society can become smaller once and remain capable. A society that becomes smaller every generation eventually has to ask what it can no longer maintain.

The first consequences are ordinary. There are fewer siblings and cousins. Fewer students enter a school district. Employers compete for a thinner pool of young workers. A smaller number of adult children care for a larger number of parents and grandparents. Pension systems designed around broad younger generations begin leaning on narrower ones. None of these problems arrives as civilization-ending news. They arrive as staffing shortages, longer drives, delayed care, higher taxes, closed buildings, and families stretched a little further.

Then the horizon lengthens. The generation missing from a classroom is also the generation that will not become thirty-year-old parents, forty-year-old engineers, fifty-year-old doctors, sixty-year-old teachers, or seventy-year-old grandparents. One smaller cohort produces the next smaller pool of potential parents. A rich country can live for a long time on capital, infrastructure, knowledge, and institutions inherited from larger generations. Eventually inheritance needs heirs.

That is the civilizational problem at the center of this book. Human beings do more than consume resources. We repair machines, raise children, keep old languages alive, build bridges, discover medicines, care for the dying, compose music, improve crops, start companies, defend countries, restore forests, and occasionally decide to send a spacecraft somewhere no one has been. Every solution to a human problem has so far required a human being somewhere in the chain.

I don't assume that more people automatically produce a better civilization. They plainly do not. Numbers cannot substitute for freedom, virtue, competence, trust, or good institutions. My concern is more basic. Civilization is a relay. Each generation receives a world it did not build and decides what to repair, discard, improve, and hand forward. Persistent demographic contraction narrows the pool of people available to receive it.

The danger is easy to miss because it moves slowly. That is also the hopeful part. A sudden disaster gives people little time to understand it. Fertility decline unfolds over decades. We can see it, argue about it, adapt to the aging that is already underway, and try to change the conditions that produced it.

The harder question is whether we will.
