A fertility rate can summarize millions of births in one decimal place, which is useful and also easy to overread. A national rate cannot tell us whether people had the children they wanted or how many postponed births may still occur later. I will use demographic statistics throughout this book, so a few distinctions are worth keeping in view.
{: .lede}

The total fertility rate is a period measure. It combines the age-specific fertility rates observed in a particular year and asks how many children a hypothetical woman would have if she experienced those rates throughout her reproductive life. [Eurostat's definition](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/3433488/5585916/KS-SF-13-013-EN.PDF/a812b080-7ede-41a4-97ef-589ee767c581) is careful on this point: the number describes the pattern of a period, not the completed family size of an actual woman.

That makes total fertility useful for seeing change quickly, but it also makes the measure sensitive to timing. If many people postpone births during a recession or a broad shift toward later parenthood, the annual rate can fall even if some of those births eventually occur; demographers call this a tempo effect. If the postponed births never happen, the decline shows up later in completed family size as well.

Completed or cohort fertility follows women born in the same period and counts the children they actually have by the end of their reproductive years. It gives us a firmer answer about lifetime family size, but only after waiting long enough for the cohort to finish having children. Period fertility is the faster reading. Cohort fertility is the finished history.

Replacement fertility is another shorthand I will use often. In a low-mortality population it is usually around 2.1 births per woman, though it is not a universal constant; a [UN technical account](https://www.un.org/en/development/desa/population/publications/pdf/fertility/Fertility-levels-and-trends_WPP2012.pdf) explains why the exact level varies. When I use 2.1 in the book, I mean a practical benchmark for many wealthy, low-mortality societies, not a moral target written into nature.

## Averages hide different lives

Two countries can report the same fertility rate for very different reasons: one may have many couples stopping after one child, another may have more people never marrying, and a third may be experiencing postponed births that partly recover later.

The same caution applies when moving from demographic patterns to motives. If fertility falls while housing costs rise, that does not by itself prove that housing caused the decline; if religious people have more children on average, belief may be only part of the explanation. Correlations tell us where to look. They do not relieve us of the work of figuring out what happened.

Stated preferences need care too. The [UNFPA/YouGov survey](https://www.unfpa.org/swp2025) found that nearly one in five reproductive-age adults in 14 countries expected not to achieve their desired number of children. That is good evidence that constrained family aspirations are real, but it is not a global census of private hopes, and desired family size can change over time.

I will therefore avoid treating every missing birth as an injustice or every observed outcome as a free preference. Some people do not want children; others want them and cannot have them. The same fertility rate can contain both lives.

## Forecasts are conditional

Population projections are especially tempting to overread because they extend a clean line into the future. Near-term projections have considerable inertia—most of the people who will be alive twenty or thirty years from now have already been born—but farther out, small changes in fertility and migration compound until the range of plausible futures becomes much wider.

The [UN methodology for its 2024 projections](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/world-population-prospects-2024-methodology-report) makes those assumptions visible. The model advances populations by age and sex, adds births, subtracts deaths, and accounts for migration; it then estimates a central path and uncertainty around it.

That central path is not a promise. In 2013, the UN assessed the probability that global population growth would end during this century at about 30 percent; the [2024 revision puts that probability at 80 percent](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/world-population-prospects-2024-summary-results-0), largely because fertility fell faster than expected in several populous countries. Projections change because behavior changes.

I will use projections to understand direction and plausible scale. When an argument depends on a particular fertility path, I will say so; when the distant future is only illustrative arithmetic, I will treat it that way rather than pretending to know how many people will be alive centuries from now.

This caution does not make the demographic turn less real. We do not need to know the population of Japan in 2180 to know that decades of very low fertility change the number of children and eventually the number of parents. The uncertainty lies mostly in how societies respond and whether fertility changes, not in the direction of the arithmetic if it does not.

Numbers are unusually helpful in a subject that unfolds too slowly to see in a day. They let us notice a smaller cohort before it becomes a closed school or a family with one adult child caring for two aging parents; they also stop at the edge of what can be counted. A fertility rate cannot measure confidence in the future or the value of a person who has not yet been born.

The next chapter begins with the history behind the numbers: how the old fear of relentless population growth gave way, within a few generations, to a world in which low fertility is becoming normal.
